\begin{abstract}
The Internet of Things (IoT) demands lightweight and secure authentication
for resource-constrained multihop networks, while also protecting the privacy of individual devices.  Existing distributed schemes reduce communication overhead on intermediate nodes but fail to provide device
anonymity—real identifiers are exposed on every authentication transaction, enabling adversarial tracking across sessions.  This paper presents a lightweight, anonymous, and decoupled distributed authentication scheme for multihop IoT networks.  Gateway-selected authentication servers (which may
be parent or non-parent nodes) authenticate newly joined devices using a two-round protocol that separates the process of authentication from session key establishment.  Device anonymity is preserved through per-session pseudonym rotation: a rotating pseudonym replaces the real device identity in all open-channel communications, preventing long-term tracking.  A dual-state synchronization mechanism maintained at the authentication
server enables seamless recovery from packet-loss events without re-authenticate.  Physically Unclonable Function (PUF) responses eliminate long-term secret storage, and a lightweight hash-based accumulator with XOR operations minimize computational overhead. Securityis validated formally (ProVerif) and informally. In addition, performance analysis is conducted through theoretical and simulation-based frameworks, focusing on energy consumption, CPU time, and communication
overhead. Analysis demonstrates that the proposed scheme is secure while maintaining a lightweight structure over existing
methods.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

The Internet of Things (IoT) connects millions of smart devices that collect
and transmit data to centralized platforms~\cite{zhou2021iot,kang2017iae}
such as gateways, often through multihop paths~\cite{lajnef2022wsn}.
Industrial and outdoor deployments expose devices to a range of adversarial
threats, making authentication a prerequisite for any communication session.
Because IoT nodes are battery-powered and resource-constrained,
authentication schemes must be \emph{lightweight} as well as
secure~\cite{alnahari2021auth,khatua2024puf}.

Centralized schemes route all authentication traffic through a gateway,
imposing excessive relay load on intermediate nodes in a multihop
topology~\cite{sheik2023auth,he2023lightweight,roy2025design}.  Distributed
schemes distribute this responsibility; however, a plain parent-node scheme
overloads whichever node a set of new devices happened to join under, while
all other capable nodes remain idle~\cite{rosa2023lightsae}.

A further deficiency shared by all existing lightweight schemes is the
\emph{absence of device anonymity}.  Every scheme in the literature
transmits the real device identity $ID_D$ on the open channel during
authentication, allowing an adversary with radio access to track a device
across sessions.  Privacy regulations such as GDPR require that device
identities not be linkable across time, a property entirely absent from
prior IoT authentication work.

This paper proposes a \emph{lightweight, anonymous, and decoupled
distributed authentication} scheme that addresses all three gaps
simultaneously:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Decoupling:} A gateway-selected authentication server (AS)
    may be any capable node—parent or non-parent—preventing skewed load on
    parent nodes.
  \item \textbf{Anonymity:} A rotating pseudonym $\mathsf{PID} =
    H(ID_D \| m_\mathsf{curr})$, refreshed after each successful key
    exchange, replaces $ID_D$ in all open-channel messages.  The gateway
    never learns the real device identity.
  \item \textbf{Desynchronization resilience:} A dual-state mechanism
    ($\mathsf{PID}_\mathsf{curr}$, $\mathsf{PID}_\mathsf{old}$,
    $m_\mathsf{curr}$, $m_\mathsf{old}$) at both the AS and the gateway
    tolerates single-packet loss without re-enrolment.
\end{itemize}

The scheme also incorporates a PUF-based secret derivation mechanism
(eliminating long-term key storage), a hash-based one-way accumulator for
group-membership verification, and timestamp-plus-session-random replay
protection.  Security is validated through ProVerif formal analysis and
informal argument.

\section{Literature Survey}

Authentication in IoT multihop networks is complicated by constrained
devices, multi-hop exposure, and the need for scalability.

\textbf{Distributed schemes.}
Rosa~et~al.~\cite{rosa2023lightsae} (LightSAE) authorize parent nodes as
authentication servers for their child nodes; however, a popular parent is
overwhelmed by simultaneous join requests from many children.
Zheng~et~al.~\cite{zheng2022puf} designed a peer-to-peer PUF-based
authentication without a centralized authority, yet the scheme lacks a
multihop decoupled design.
Alruwaili~et~al.~\cite{alruwaili2025raaf} deployed multiple edge servers
with PUF, but the absence of a genuine multihop topology means certain
servers still receive disproportionate load.

\textbf{PUF-based schemes.}
Li~et~al.~\cite{li2020puf} and Yang~et~al.~\cite{yang2024sakms} combine
PUF-based authentication with session key exchange to avoid long-term secret
storage; both incur high computational overhead due to ECC operations.
He~et~al.~\cite{he2023lightweight} achieve lightweight anonymous
authentication using hash-and-XOR primitives for CoAP/OSCORE at the cost of
a centralized server.

\textbf{Identity-hiding schemes.}
Al-Riyami and Paterson~\cite{alriyami2003cert} eliminate certificate storage
via ECC-based certificateless cryptography; the ECC operations are too heavy
for resource-constrained IoT nodes.
Blockchain-based schemes~\cite{zhang2023blockchain,ahmed2023chains,sikarwar2020eff}
achieve decentralization but incur prohibitive storage and consensus overhead.

\section{Prerequisites}

\subsection{Physically Unclonable Function (PUF)}

PUF operates as a one-way function performing a mapping of a challenge $C$
to a unique response $R$ from a pool of challenge space $\mathbb{C}$.
This uniqueness stems from the inherent hardware variation in IoT nodes,
which is infeasible to clone or replicate.
To recognize a function as a valid PUF, the following criteria must hold:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Evaluatable}: A node embedded with a legitimate PUF can
    efficiently calculate the response: $R = \mathsf{PUF}(C)$,
    $\forall\, C \in \mathbb{C}$.
  \item \emph{Unpredictability}: An attacker node without access to a
    legitimate PUF cannot guess $R \in \mathbb{R}$ from a challenge
    $C \in \mathbb{C}$ better than a random:
    $\Pr[R = \mathsf{PUF}(C)] \approx 1/|\mathbb{R}|$.
  \item \emph{Uniqueness}: Two nodes with different PUF instances
    ($\mathsf{PUF}_1$ and $\mathsf{PUF}_2$) never produce the same
    response for a given challenge $C$:
    $\Pr[\mathsf{PUF}_1(C) = \mathsf{PUF}_2(C)] \approx 0$.
  \item \emph{Reliability}: A node embedded with a PUF produces the same
    response $R$ for the same challenge $C$, even at different times:
    $(R_1 = \mathsf{PUF}(C)\ \&\ R_2 = \mathsf{PUF}(C)) \Rightarrow R_1 = R_2$.
  \item \emph{One-Wayness}: The inversion of a given response $R$ to obtain
    the challenge $C$ is infeasible:
    $\nexists\,(\mathsf{PUF}^{-1}(R) = C)$.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Hash-Based One-Way Accumulator}

A hash-based accumulator is a lightweight cryptographic
construct~\cite{weerasena2024acc}, where multiple elements are hashed and
combined in a single compact value.  It allows individual elements to prove
their membership within the accumulated value without showing other values.
In a one-way accumulator, the order of accumulating input elements does not
alter the final combined value.  This is because of its quasi-commutative
property, which is defined by a function $f: A \times B \Rightarrow A$,
such that, for all $a \in A$, and $b, c \in B$: $f(a,\, f(b, c)) = f(f(a, b),\, c)$.
The above equation implies that the accumulation order does not impact the
final value, where $a$ is considered to be the seed.  A formal definition
of the hash-based one-way accumulator is as follows.  Let
$Y = \{y_1, y_2, \ldots, y_m\}$ be a set of elements, where each element
$y_i$ is hashed ($\forall\, i \in [1, m]$) as $Y_i = H(y_i)$.
These hashed values are accumulated into one compact value, where $s$ is the
seed and $\mathbin{\&}$ is the bitwise AND operation: $H(s, Y) = s \mathbin{\&} \textstyle\bigwedge_{i=1}^{m} Y_i$.
During membership verification, the hash of the individual element is
recomputed and checked as $H(s, Y) \mathbin{\&} H(y_i) = H(s, Y)$.
If the above equation holds, then $y_i$ is a member of the accumulated
value $H(s, Y)$.

\subsection{Pseudonym-Based Device Anonymity}

A \emph{pseudonym} is a session-scoped, unlinkable identifier derived from
the real identity.  In this work, $\mathsf{PID} = H(ID_D \| m_\mathsf{curr})$,
where $m_\mathsf{curr}$ is a session-based random renewed after every
successful key exchange.  Because $H$ is collision-resistant and
$m_\mathsf{curr}$ is unpredictable to an observer, successive pseudonyms
are computationally unlinkable.  The real identity $ID_D$ is transmitted only once, over a
secure enrolment channel, and never appears on the open network again.

\section{Base Scheme: Overview and Limitations}
\label{sec:base}

The proposed scheme extends the lightweight decoupled distributed
authentication scheme of Das and Khatua~\cite{das2026comsnets}, referred to
hereafter as the \emph{base scheme}.  This section briefly summarises the
base scheme and identifies the two gaps that motivate our work.

\subsection{System Model}

The base scheme operates in a multihop IoT network where the gateway $GW$
pre-selects a set $\mathcal{AS}' = \{AS_1, \ldots, AS_n\}$ of
authentication servers.  Each $AS \in \mathcal{AS}'$ shares a long-term key
$K_{GW\text{-}AS}$ with $GW$ and may be a parent or non-parent of the newly
joined device $D$, decoupling authentication from network topology.  IoT
nodes embed a PUF and support symmetric encryption/decryption, XOR, hashing,
and bitwise AND.

\subsection{Protocol Phases}

\textbf{Phase 1 — Node Enrolment (secure channel).}
$AS$ issues a PUF challenge $c_D$ and session-based random $m_D$ to $D$.  $D$
runs $R_D = \mathsf{PUF}_D(c_D)$, generates a group secret $y_D$, and
returns $(y_D, R_D, c_{AS\text{-}D})$ to $AS$ over the secure channel.
$AS$ updates the accumulator $T_\mathsf{Acc} = T_\mathsf{Acc}
\mathbin{\&} H(y_D)$ and stores $\Phi_{AS\text{-}D} =
\mathsf{PUF}_{AS}(c_{AS\text{-}D}) \oplus R_D$.

\textbf{Phase 2 — Node Authentication (open channel).}
$D$ sends its \emph{real identity} $ID_D$, masked membership tag
$Y_{AS\text{-}D}^H = H(y_D) \oplus H(R_D \| m_D \| ID_D \| ts_1)$,
and timestamp $ts_1$ directly on the open channel.  $AS$ reconstructs
$R_D'$ via $\Phi_{AS\text{-}D}$, recovers $H(y_D)$, and verifies
group membership:
$T_\mathsf{Acc} \mathbin{\&} H(y_D) \stackrel{?}{=} T_\mathsf{Acc}$.

\textbf{Phase 3 — Session Key Exchange (open channel).}
$AS$ generates $m_\mathsf{new} = H(n_1)$, derives $K_{GW\text{-}D} =
H(R_D' \| m_\mathsf{new})$, and simultaneously delivers: (i)~masked
$m_H = m_\mathsf{new} \oplus H(Y_D^{H'} \| m_D \| R_D' \| ID_{AS} \|
ID_D \| ts_2)$ to $D$, and (ii)~an AES-encrypted token
$\mathsf{SE}(K_{GW\text{-}AS}, ID_D \| ID_{AS} \| K_{GW\text{-}D} \|
ts_\mathsf{auth})$ to $GW$.  $D$ unmasks $m_\mathsf{new}$ and derives
$K_{GW\text{-}D} = H(R_D \| m_\mathsf{new})$.

\subsection{Identified Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

Despite being efficient and lightweight, the base scheme has two notable
gaps that limit its applicability in privacy-sensitive deployments.

\textbf{L1 — No Device Anonymity.}
The real device identity $ID_D$ appears in every open-channel authentication
message (Phase~2) and inside the gateway token (Phase~3).  An adversary
monitoring the wireless medium can trivially link all sessions belonging to a
device, enabling persistent cross-session tracking — a direct violation of
modern privacy requirements such as GDPR.

\textbf{L2 — No Desynchronization Recovery.}
Both $AS$ and $D$ maintain only a single session state ($m_D$).  If the
Phase~3 delivery of $m_H$ to $D$ is lost due to packet loss, $D$ retains
the old $m_D$ while $AS$ has already advanced to $m_\mathsf{new}$.  Their
session states diverge, causing all subsequent authentication attempts to
fail until the device re-enrols — an energy-costly operation for
battery-powered nodes in a multihop network.

These two gaps are precisely what the proposed scheme addresses through
pseudonym rotation (Section~\ref{sec:proposed}) and dual-state management.

\section{Proposed Authentication Scheme}
\label{sec:proposed}

\subsection{System Model and Assumptions}
\label{sec:sysmodel}

The proposed scheme is designed to help nodes establish secure multihop
communications with the gateway.  To establish a secure communication, nodes
should be authenticated by a trusted authentication server.  A set of
existing IoT nodes inside the network is entrusted with the responsibility of
authentication by the gateway in a distributed manner.  The gateway also
shares a secret key with each of those responsible IoT
nodes/authentication servers.  The authentication server can be a parent or
a non-parent node—they may be \emph{decoupled}—which prevents newly joined
child nodes from overwhelming their parent with biased authentication load.

IoT nodes are assumed to be embedded with a PUF and are capable of performing
various cryptographic operations, such as encryption/decryption, XOR,
hashing, bitwise AND, and concatenation.

Nodes $D$ and $AS$ are assumed to be the newly joined node and its
authentication server, respectively.  $GW$ represents the gateway
symbolically.  $AS$ may be located at a singlehop or multihop distance from
$D$.  It is assumed that $GW$ has already selected the authentication server
set and has established a shared secret key with each member of the set.
Let $\mathcal{AS}' = \{AS_1, AS_2, \ldots, AS_n\}$ be the set of
authentication servers selected by $GW$, such that $AS \in \mathcal{AS}'$.
$GW$ has a shared secret key $K_{GW\text{-}AS}$ with $AS$.  It is also
assumed that $D$, whose distinct identifier is $ID_D$, possesses the address
of its authentication server $AS$ along with its unique identity $ID_{AS}$.
Table~\ref{tab:notations} elaborates symbols and functions incorporated in
the following scheme.

There are mainly three phases — node enrolment, node authentication, and node
session key exchange.  The following sections elaborate on these phases.

\subsection{Node Enrolment Phase}

The main purpose of this phase is to enrol the unique secret of $D$ (i.e.,
$y_D$) with the accumulated value ($T_\mathsf{Acc}$) of $AS$.  During the
authentication phase, $D$ uses $y_D$ again to prove its group membership.
PUF responses and a session-based random are also exchanged to secure the
communication of $y_D$ during authentication.  Additionally, the phase
initialises the pseudonym and dual-state records that protect $D$'s identity
on all subsequent open-channel exchanges.  This phase is assumed to be
executed once, over an end-to-end encrypted channel, for every newly joined
node.  Figure~\ref{fig:enrolment} details the message flow.

\begin{figure}[!t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{fig_enrollment}
  \caption{Phase 1 — Node Enrolment Phase (secure channel).}
  \label{fig:enrolment}
\end{figure}

$D$ initiates the phase by sending a registration request along with its
unique identity $ID_D$ to $AS$—the only time $ID_D$ appears in any network
message.  As a response, $AS$ generates a challenge $c_D \in \mathbb{C}_D$
and a session-based random $m_D$, and forwards them securely to $D$.  Both
credentials secure the communication during authentication: $c_D$ enables
$D$ to regenerate its PUF response, while $m_D$ introduces unpredictability
into open-channel messages, preventing adversarial prediction.  With $c_D$
as input to its embedded PUF, $D$ obtains $R_D = \mathsf{PUF}_D(c_D)$.
$D$ also generates its unique secret $y_D$ (a random value); the security
of the entire scheme critically depends on maintaining the confidentiality
of $y_D$.  $D$ picks a reverse challenge $c_{AS\text{-}D}$ and sends
$(y_D, R_D, c_{AS\text{-}D})$ back to $AS$ over the secure channel,
maintaining the confidentiality of $y_D$.  $D$ stores
$\{y_D,\, c_D,\, m_\mathsf{curr}{=}m_D\}$.

$AS$ inputs $y_D$ to the hash function $H(\cdot)$, generating the tag
$Y_D^H = H(y_D)$, then performs a bitwise AND with the current accumulated
value: $T_\mathsf{Acc} = T_\mathsf{Acc} \mathbin{\&} Y_D^H$.  The
accumulated value contains the compact, hashed, unique secrets of all
enrolled nodes, which are authenticated by $AS$.  In this way, $D$
successfully enrols itself with $AS$.  $AS$ then generates
$R_{AS\text{-}D} = \mathsf{PUF}_{AS}(c_{AS\text{-}D})$ and calculates
$\Phi_{AS\text{-}D} = R_{AS\text{-}D} \oplus R_D$, keeping $R_D$ in
XOR-protected form for further secure communications.

To protect $D$'s identity in all subsequent open-channel messages, $AS$
initialises a per-session pseudonym record.  Since $ID_D$ must not appear
on the open channel after enrolment, $D$ will instead be identified by the
\emph{pseudonym} $\mathsf{PID} = H(ID_D \| m_\mathsf{curr})$ in all future
authentication messages.  Because $H$ is one-way and $m_\mathsf{curr}$ is
private, this pseudonym cannot be linked to $ID_D$ or to pseudonyms from
other sessions by a channel observer.  $AS$ pre-computes
$\mathsf{PID}_\mathsf{curr} = H(ID_D \| m_\mathsf{curr})$ and sets
$\mathsf{PID}_\mathsf{old} = \perp$, $m_\mathsf{old} = \perp$.  The
\emph{dual state} ($m_\mathsf{curr}$, $m_\mathsf{old}$,
$\mathsf{PID}_\mathsf{curr}$, $\mathsf{PID}_\mathsf{old}$) is maintained
because Phase~3 delivers the updated session random $m_\mathsf{new}$ over
the open channel, where a single packet loss can cause $D$ to retain
$m_\mathsf{curr}$ while $AS$ has already advanced.  By retaining both the
current and the previous pseudonym, $AS$ can recover from such
desynchronisation without requiring $D$ to re-authenticate.  $AS$ stores
$\{T_\mathsf{Acc},\, ID_D,\, \Phi_{AS\text{-}D},\, c_{AS\text{-}D},\,
m_\mathsf{curr},\, m_\mathsf{old},\, \mathsf{PID}_\mathsf{curr},\,
\mathsf{PID}_\mathsf{old}\}$.

\subsection{Node Authentication Phase}

The main purpose of this phase is to prove the membership of $D$'s unique
secret $y_D$ to $AS$.  Since authentication is executed over the open
channel, $y_D$ must be communicated in a secure form.  Both the PUF-based
challenge-response, producing the secret $R_D$, and the session-based random
$m_\mathsf{curr}$, incorporating unpredictability, are known only to $D$ and
$AS$.  Additionally, the proposed scheme replaces the real identity $ID_D$
with the current pseudonym $\mathsf{PID}$, so that a passive observer cannot
link successive sessions to the same device.
Figure~\ref{fig:auth} illustrates the phase.

\begin{figure}[!t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{fig_auth}
  \caption{Phase 2 — Node Authentication Phase (open channel).}
  \label{fig:auth}
\end{figure}

The phase is initiated by $D$.  $D$ regenerates $R_D = \mathsf{PUF}_D(c_D)$
from the stored challenge $c_D$, obtained during enrolment.  Rather than
including its real identity $ID_D$—which would enable cross-session
tracking—$D$ computes its current pseudonym $\mathsf{PID} = H(ID_D \| m_\mathsf{curr})$.
$D$ then produces the hashed secret $Y_D^H = H(y_D)$ in XOR-masked form:
$Y_{AS\text{-}D}^H = H(y_D) \oplus H(R_D \| m_\mathsf{curr} \| \mathsf{PID} \| ts_1)$.
The XOR mask is the hash of $R_D$ (regenerated from $c_D$), the
session-based random $m_\mathsf{curr}$, the pseudonym $\mathsf{PID}$, and
the current timestamp $ts_1$.  The timestamp provides replay protection to
the XOR-masked $Y_D^H$.  The complete message $(\mathsf{PID},\,
Y_{AS\text{-}D}^H,\, ts_1)$ is sent to $AS$.  An attacker cannot obtain
$H(y_D)$ from $Y_{AS\text{-}D}^H$ without knowing $R_D$ (which can only be
generated by $D$'s PUF) and $m_\mathsf{curr}$ (known only to $D$ and $AS$).

After receiving the message, $AS$ performs a \emph{dual-state lookup}: it
checks whether the received $\mathsf{PID}$ matches $\mathsf{PID}_\mathsf{curr}$
or $\mathsf{PID}_\mathsf{old}$ in its records.  A match on
$\mathsf{PID}_\mathsf{old}$ indicates that $D$ missed the Phase~3 delivery
in the previous session and still holds the prior $m_\mathsf{curr}$; $AS$
then uses the stored $m_\mathsf{old}$ for this verification, recovering from
the desynchronisation without requiring re-enrolment.  $AS$ verifies
the freshness of $ts_1$.  $R_D'$ is calculated as $\Phi_{AS\text{-}D} \oplus
\mathsf{PUF}_{AS}(c_{AS\text{-}D})$, where both $\Phi_{AS\text{-}D}$ and
$c_{AS\text{-}D}$ are retrieved from $D$'s enrolment record.  $AS$
regenerates the same XOR mask and unmasks $Y_D^{H'}$, then performs the
bitwise AND check: $T_\mathsf{Acc}^\mathsf{new} = T_\mathsf{Acc} \mathbin{\&} Y_D^{H'} \stackrel{?}{=} T_\mathsf{Acc}$.
From the properties of the hash-based one-way accumulator, if an element is
combined into an accumulated value using bitwise AND and the same operation is
again performed between them, it gives back the same accumulated value.  If
$T_\mathsf{Acc}^\mathsf{new} = T_\mathsf{Acc}$, it proves that the unique
secret of $D$ is already a legitimate member of $AS$, completing
authentication.  $AS$ replies with $(\mathsf{ACK},\, ts_2)$ and proceeds to
Phase~3.

\subsection{Node Session Key Exchange Phase}

This phase is executed by $AS$ to inform $GW$ about the successful
authentication of the newly joined node $D$ and to mediate the session key
between $GW$ and $D$.  In addition to the base session-key mechanism, the
proposed scheme derives a fresh pseudonym $\mathsf{PID}_\mathsf{new}$ for
$D$ at the end of each session and updates the dual-state records at both
$AS$ and $GW$, ensuring that successive sessions are unlinkable.  The two
deliveries to $D$ and to $GW$ are sent simultaneously.
Figure~\ref{fig:keyex} shows the phase.

\begin{figure}[!t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{fig_keyex}
  \caption{Phase 3 — Node Session Key Exchange Phase (open channel, simultaneous delivery).}
  \label{fig:keyex}
\end{figure}

$AS$ uses $\mathsf{auth\_token}_D$ to deliver the session key $K_{GW\text{-}D}$
to $GW$.  $K_{GW\text{-}D}$ is computed as $H(R_D' \| m_\mathsf{new})$,
where $R_D'$ is the PUF response of $D$ regenerated by $AS$ during the
authentication phase and thus known only to $AS$ and $D$, and
$m_\mathsf{new} = H(n_1)$ is derived from a fresh random $n_1$ generated by
$AS$.  The computed $m_\mathsf{new}$ also serves as the updated session-based
random for the next authentication session between $AS$ and $D$,
incorporating an update to the authentication secret.  In addition, $AS$
derives the session key $K_{GW\text{-}D} = H(R_D' \| m_\mathsf{new})$ and
the next pseudonym $\mathsf{PID}_\mathsf{new} = H(ID_D \| m_\mathsf{new})$.
Because $\mathsf{PID}_\mathsf{new}$ is based on the fresh $m_\mathsf{new}$,
it is computationally unlinkable to the $\mathsf{PID}$ used in the current
session.  The session key, $ID_{AS}$, $ID_D$, the next pseudonym
$\mathsf{PID}_\mathsf{new}$, and the authentication completion timestamp
$ts_\mathsf{auth}$ are encrypted under $K_{GW\text{-}AS}$ to form
$\mathsf{auth\_token}_D$.  The token carries $\mathsf{PID}_\mathsf{new}$ so
that $GW$ can update its pseudonym mapping for $D$'s future communications.

$AS$ also informs $D$ of $m_\mathsf{new}$ so that $D$ can independently
derive $K_{GW\text{-}D}$ and its next pseudonym.  $AS$ masks $m_\mathsf{new}$
to protect it from adversaries—if an adversary captures $m_\mathsf{new}$, the
probability of predicting future session key material increases—so it is
masked as $m_H = m_\mathsf{new} \oplus H(Y_D^{H'} \| m_\mathsf{curr} \| R_D' \| ID_{AS} \| \mathsf{PID}_\mathsf{curr} \| ts_2)$.
Here, $Y_D^{H'}$ is the hashed group secret known to $AS$ from the
authentication phase; $m_\mathsf{curr}$ and $R_D'$ are likewise known from
authentication; and $ts_2$ prevents replay of $m_H$ in a future session.
$AS$ simultaneously sends $(m_H,\, ts_2)$ to $D$ and $(ID_{AS},\,
\mathsf{auth\_token}_D)$ to $GW$, then advances its own dual state:
$m_\mathsf{old} \leftarrow m_\mathsf{curr}$, $m_\mathsf{curr} \leftarrow
m_\mathsf{new}$, $\mathsf{PID}_\mathsf{old} \leftarrow
\mathsf{PID}_\mathsf{curr}$, $\mathsf{PID}_\mathsf{curr} \leftarrow
\mathsf{PID}_\mathsf{new}$.

Obtaining $m_H$, $D$ verifies the freshness of $ts_2$ and recovers
$m_\mathsf{new}$ by regenerating the same hash mask and XOR-ing with $m_H$;
all inputs ($Y_D^H$, $R_D$, $m_\mathsf{curr}$, $ID_{AS}$, $\mathsf{PID}$,
$ts_2$) are already known to $D$.  $D$ establishes the session key
$K_{GW\text{-}D} = H(R_D \| m_\mathsf{new})$, updates its pseudonym
$\mathsf{PID}_\mathsf{curr} = H(ID_D \| m_\mathsf{new})$, advances
$m_\mathsf{curr} \leftarrow m_\mathsf{new}$, and stores $K_{GW\text{-}D}$.

$GW$ decrypts the token with $K_{GW\text{-}AS}$, verifies $ts_\mathsf{auth}$,
and extracts $K_{GW\text{-}D}$ and $\mathsf{PID}_\mathsf{new}$.  $GW$ updates
its pseudonym record: $\mathsf{PID}_\mathsf{old} \leftarrow
\mathsf{PID}_\mathsf{curr}$, $\mathsf{PID}_\mathsf{curr} \leftarrow
\mathsf{PID}_\mathsf{new}$, and stores the session keyed by both.
Retaining $\mathsf{PID}_\mathsf{old}$ ensures that if the Phase~3 delivery
to $D$ was lost, $GW$ can still service $D$'s data frames under the prior
session key pending re-authentication.

\section{Security Analysis}

\subsection{Formal Security Analysis}

The proposed scheme is translated into ProVerif~\cite{blanchet2018proverif}
queries under the Dolev--Yao adversary model~\cite{dolev1983security}, where
the adversary controls the public channel and can intercept, replay, modify,
and inject messages.  Nineteen queries are verified; all are satisfied.
Figure~\ref{fig:proverif} shows the verification summary.

\subsubsection{Protocol Correspondence (Q1--Q9)}

\begin{figure}[!t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{fig_proverif}
  \caption{ProVerif verification output.}
  \label{fig:proverif}
\end{figure}

Nine injective and non-injective correspondence queries are verified.
Queries~1--2 cover enrollment: \texttt{DeviceEnrolled}~$\Rightarrow$~\texttt{DeviceEnrollmentStarts} and
\texttt{ASEnrollmentCompletes}~$\Rightarrow$~\texttt{ASEnrollmentStarts}, ensuring enrollment messages
cannot be replayed to forge a false completion.
Queries~3--6 cover the two authentication rounds from both principals'
perspectives: $\mathsf{inj\text{-}event}(\mathsf{DeviceAuthRound1Done}(ID_D,ID_{AS}))
\Rightarrow \mathsf{inj\text{-}event}(\mathsf{DeviceAuthStarts}(ID_D,ID_{AS}))$,
and identically for $\mathsf{ASAuthCompletes}$, $\mathsf{DeviceKeyExDone}$,
and $\mathsf{ASKeyExCompletes}$, confirming that each round completes only if
the same entity initiated it—preventing replay and MITM in both rounds.
Query~7 verifies the \emph{cross-round credential binding}: the PUF response
$R_D$ and session seed $m_D$ bound in Round~1 are the same values present in
Round~2, ruling out cross-session credential mixing.  Queries~8--9 cover the
post-authentication path: the gateway receives a forwarded token only if $AS$
actually sent it (injective, no token forgery), and the gateway accepts device
data only if the device transmitted it (non-injective data-plane authenticity).

\subsubsection{Secrecy and Forward Secrecy (Q10--Q16)}

The session key $K_{GW\text{-}D} = H(R_D \| m_\mathsf{new})$ is confirmed
unreachable by the adversary from all three principals' perspectives
independently (Q10--Q12): $\mathsf{query}\ \mathsf{attacker}(\mathsf{SecretK\_GW\_D\_Device}[])\;\Rightarrow\;\mathtt{false}$,
and equivalently for \texttt{SecretK\_GW\_D\_AS} and \texttt{SecretK\_GW\_D\_GW}.
Q13 additionally confirms that the rotated session seed $m_\mathsf{new} =
H(n_1)$ is secret: an adversary who compromises a session key cannot recover
$m_\mathsf{new}$ to derive future keys, establishing forward secrecy.  Q14
confirms that the PUF response $R_D$ is never attacker-reachable, preserving
PUF unclonability.  Q15 confirms that the real device identity $ID_D$—wrapped
in \texttt{SecretID\_D} and never sent in plaintext after enrollment—is
secret, formally establishing device anonymity.  Q16 confirms that the
AS-issued nonce $ts_2$ is secret until used in Round~2, protecting freshness
of the key-exchange request.

\subsubsection{Offline Guessing Resistance (Q17--Q19)}

Three weak-secrecy assertions verify that $K_{GW\text{-}D}$ cannot be guessed
offline from the public transcript from the device, AS, and GW perspectives
respectively.  \emph{Weak secrecy} in ProVerif means that no adversary can
distinguish $K_{GW\text{-}D}$ from a random value even given unlimited
dictionary power against the public transcript, ruling out offline brute-force
and dictionary attacks against the session key.

\subsection{Informal Analysis}

\subsubsection{Replay Attack Resistance}
Phase~1 is executed over a secure channel and immune to replay.  Phases~2
and~3 embed timestamps ($ts_1$, $ts_2$, $ts_\mathsf{auth}$) and the
session-based random $m_\mathsf{curr}$ into the hash masks.  A replayed
message with a stale timestamp or stale $m_\mathsf{curr}$ will fail the
freshness check at $AS$, $D$, or $GW$ respectively.

\subsubsection{MITM Attack Resistance}
Phase~1 is conducted over an encrypted channel; MITM is precluded.  In
Phases~2--3, the PUF response $R_D$ (device-specific, unreproducible) and
session random $m_\mathsf{curr}$ (private to $D$ and $AS$) are embedded in
every XOR mask.  Without $R_D$ and the current $m_\mathsf{curr}$, no
adversary can forge a valid masked tag $Y_{AS\text{-}D}^H$ or unmask $m_H$.

\subsubsection{Device Anonymity}
$ID_D$ is transmitted only once, over the secure enrolment channel, and is
never exposed on the open network again.  All open-channel messages use
$\mathsf{PID} = H(ID_D \| m_\mathsf{curr})$, which is refreshed to
$H(ID_D \| m_\mathsf{new})$ after every key exchange.  Because $m_\mathsf{new}$
is derived from a freshly generated nonce $n_1$, successive pseudonyms are
computationally unlinkable.

\subsubsection{Desynchronization Recovery}
If the Phase~3 simultaneous delivery to $D$ fails (packet loss), $D$ retains
its $m_\mathsf{curr}$ unchanged while $AS$ has already advanced to
$m_\mathsf{new}$.  On the next authentication attempt, $D$ computes its PID
from the stale $m_\mathsf{curr}$, which equals $\mathsf{PID}_\mathsf{old}$
at $AS$.  $AS$ recognizes it via the dual-state lookup and resumes from the
consistent prior state without re-enrolment.  $GW$ similarly retains the
mapping $\mathsf{PID}_\mathsf{old} \to K_{GW\text{-}D}$ for session
continuity.  Figure~\ref{fig:desync} illustrates the two outcomes.

is 9.59\,mJ, of which only 2.25\,mJ is attributed to the
authentication-and-key-exchange phases repeated at each session.  This
makes the scheme suitable for duty-cycled IoT devices with millijoule
energy budgets per event.

% -------------------------------------------------------
\section{Conclusion}

This paper presented a lightweight, anonymous, and decoupled distributed
authentication scheme for multihop IoT networks.  A gateway-selected
authentication server—which may or may not be the parent of the newly joined
device—performs group-membership verification (Phase~2) and session key
establishment (Phase~3) in two clean rounds.  Device anonymity is preserved
through per-session pseudonym rotation, preventing adversarial tracking across
sessions.  A dual-state mechanism at both the authentication server and the
gateway provides transparent desynchronization recovery without re-enrolment.
PUF-based authentication eliminates long-term secret storage, and
hash-based accumulator operations with XOR masking keep the computational
overhead low.

Formal verification in ProVerif confirms authentication correspondence,
session key secrecy, resistance to offline guessing attacks, and
observational-equivalence-based anonymity.  A hardware deployment on
Raspberry Pi~3B+ nodes demonstrates a 26.8\% reduction in
authentication-and-key-exchange communication overhead versus the base
scheme~\cite{das2026comsnets}, with a total three-phase energy of 9.59\,mJ
and per-authentication CPU time of 0.59\,ms, validating practical
suitability for resource-constrained multihop IoT deployments.
