\begin{abstract}
The Internet of Things (IoT) demands lightweight and secure authentication
for resource constrained devices in multihop networks, and protecting the
privacy of individual devices.  Many distributed authentication schemes have
been proposed in the past for IoT networks. Among those schemes, the decoupled
distributed authentication scheme (DAuth) has been
proposed recently for multihop IoT networks. However, we have identified
two critical gaps in that scheme. First, the real device identity is exposed on the open
channel in every authentication session, enabling adversarial cross-session
tracking. Second, packet loss in the session key establishment phase desynchronises
the device and server states, forcing full re-enrolment of the device. Therefore, this paper proposes a security improved and anonymity preserving decoupled distributed authentication scheme,
addressing both the identified gaps. Device anonymity is preserved through per-session
pseudonym rotation, where a rotating pseudonym replaces the real device identity
in all open-channel communications, preventing long-term tracking.  A
dual-state synchronisation mechanism maintained by the authentication
server enables seamless recovery from single packet-loss
events without re-enrolment of the device. The proposed scheme is evaluated via ProVerif-based
formal security analysis tool. Further, the theoretical and simulation-based performance analysis confirm that the proposed scheme reduces energy by
9.5\,\% and 32.4\,\%, and CPU time by 9.5\,\% and 32.4\,\%, over the state-of-the-art
LAAKA and Zhou et al. schemes, respectively, while maintaining the lowest overhead among all
anonymity-preserving schemes.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

The Internet of Things (IoT) connects millions of smart devices that collect
and transmit data to centralized platforms~\cite{zhou2021iot}
such as gateways, often through multihop paths~\cite{lajnef2022wsn}.
Industrial and outdoor deployments expose devices to a range of adversarial
threats, making authentication a prerequisite for any communication session.
Because IoT nodes are battery-powered and resource-constrained,
authentication schemes must be lightweight as well as
secure~\cite{khatua2024puf}.

Centralized schemes route all authentication traffic through a gateway,
imposing excessive relay load on intermediate nodes in a multihop
topology~\cite{sheik2023auth}\cite{he2023lightweight}\cite{roy2025design}.  Distributed
schemes distribute this responsibility into the parent nodes of respective child nodes or newly joined nodes~\cite{rosa2023lightsae}. However, a plain parent centric distributed authentication schemes
overload whichever parent node a set of new devices happened to join under, while
all other capable parent nodes remain idle~\cite{rosa2023lightsae}.

DAuth~\cite{das2026comsnets} addressed this skewed-authentication load problem by proposing a
lightweight decoupled distributed authentication scheme in which the gateway or root node
pre-assigns any capable IoT node (parent or non-parent) as the
authentication server for the newly joined devices.  By decoupling authentication
from network joining process, the scheme distributes the authentication load more evenly
across the network.  Authentication process in DAuth~\cite{das2026comsnets} relies on Physically Unclonable Function (PUF)-based secret derivation, a
hash-based one-way accumulator and
XOR-masked message exchanges, resulting in an efficient and lightweight authentication
protocol.

Despite its efficiency, DAuth~\cite{das2026comsnets} leaves two security gaps
unaddressed.  First, the real device identity is transmitted in
plaintext over the open channel in every authentication session, enabling an
adversary with radio access to track a device across sessions, a direct
violation of modern privacy requirements.  Second, both the authentication
server and the device maintain only a single session state, and thus a packet loss
during session key delivery desynchronises their states, causing all
subsequent authentication attempts to fail until the device undergoes full
re-enrolment, which is an energy-costly operation for resource
constrained nodes.

This paper addresses both gaps with two lightweight extensions to DAuth.  To close the anonymity gap, the
real identity is never placed on the open channel after enrolment process conducted via secure channel. The device identity is
replaced by a rotating pseudonym that is refreshed after every key exchange, so successive sessions become
unlinkable to an observer.  To eliminate the re-enrolment penalty caused by
desynchronisation, the authentication server maintains a
dual state, if the session-key update is lost in transit, the
device still arrives under its previous pseudonym, which the server recognises
and uses to re-synchronise without repeating enrolment.

This paper makes the following key contributions.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item A anonymity preserving and recoverable distributed authentication scheme is proposed that
    introduces per-session pseudonym rotation to preserve device anonymity and
    a dual-state mechanism to recover from desynchronisation without
    re-enrolment.
  \item The security of the proposed scheme is validated through both
    informal analysis and formal verification using
    ProVerif.
  \item The efficiency of the proposed scheme is evaluated using both
    theoretical and simulation-based (COOJA/Contiki-NG) frameworks.
\end{enumerate}

Section~II elaborates on related literature.  Section~III presents the
proposed scheme.  Furthermore, Section~IV contains the detailed security
analysis.  Finally, Section~V presents the performance analysis, with
Section~VI stating the conclusion and future work.

\section{Literature Survey}

Most traditional centralized authentication schemes~\cite{sheik2023auth,he2023lightweight,roy2025design}
rely on a centralized authenticator, leading to increased computational and
communication overhead in multihop networks.  Distributed authentication schemes
mitigate this drawback through multiple authenticators.  However, certain
distributed schemes~\cite{alruwaili2025raaf} suffer from skewed authentication
load on particular edge or fog servers and ignore multihop IoT considerations.
In LightSAE~\cite{rosa2023lightsae}, parent IoT nodes authenticate child nodes,
causing skewed authentication overhead on nodes with many children, while those
with fewer or no children remain underutilized.  On the other hand, traditional
peer-to-peer (P2P) schemes depend on long-term key storage, introducing security
vulnerabilities.  PUF-based P2P schemes~\cite{zheng2022puf,li2020puf} mitigate
key storage but still suffer from skewed authentication overhead on certain IoT
nodes.  Both~\cite{li2020puf,yang2024sakms} incur high computational and
communication overhead due to ECC operations.

DAuth~\cite{das2026comsnets} addresses these drawbacks by proposing a
lightweight decoupled distributed authentication scheme in which both parent
and non-parent nodes can authenticate newly joined devices, mitigating the
skewed load scenario.  It uses PUF-based secret derivation and a hash-based
one-way accumulator for membership verification without revealing device
secrets.  However, the real device identity is transmitted in plaintext over
the open channel in every authentication session, enabling an adversary to
track a device across sessions.  Additionally, both the authentication server
and the device maintain only a single session state, so a packet loss during
session key delivery desynchronises their states, forcing full re-enrolment.
Zhong~et~al.~\cite{zhong2025survey} present a systematic taxonomy of
lightweight anonymous IoT authentication, deconstructing existing protocols
into nine sub-frameworks (SF1-UA through SF9-FS) covering individual security
and privacy properties such as user anonymity, untraceability, and forward
secrecy.  These sub-frameworks are then composed into two general frameworks:
GF1, which achieves user anonymity but cannot simultaneously provide forward
secrecy and desynchronisation resistance; and GF2, which satisfies all three
properties together.  Their analysis demonstrates that this combination is
absent from prior lightweight schemes, establishing GF2 as the target
architectural pattern for a complete solution.
LAAKA~\cite{ali2024laaka} and Zhou~et~al.~\cite{zhou2024auth} address
anonymity in IoT authentication but incur higher communication overhead than
DAuth, owing to larger message payloads in both schemes and, in the case of
Zhou~et~al., an additional message exchange per session.

Therefore, this paper proposes an anonymity-preserving extension to DAuth,
incorporating two lightweight mechanisms.  Per-session pseudonym rotation
replaces the real device identity on the open channel with a refreshed
pseudonym after every key exchange, making successive sessions unlinkable to
an observer.  A dual-state mechanism maintained at the authentication
server enables seamless recovery from single packet-loss events
without re-enrolment.

As shown in Table~\ref{tab:features}, the proposed scheme is compared with
representative state-of-the-art schemes.  Existing lightweight schemes trade
off at least one of: decoupled design, PUF-based authentication,
accumulator-based membership verification, anonymity, or desynchronisation
recovery.  The proposed scheme satisfies all criteria simultaneously while
maintaining the lowest computational and communication overhead among all
anonymity-preserving schemes.

\section{Proposed Scheme}
\label{sec:proposed}

\subsection{System Model and Assumptions}
\label{sec:sysmodel}

The proposed scheme is designed to help nodes establish secure multihop
communications with the gateway.  To establish a secure communication, nodes
should be authenticated by a trusted authentication server.  A set of
existing IoT nodes inside the network is entrusted with the responsibility of
authentication by the gateway in a distributed manner.  The gateway also
shares a secret key with each of those responsible IoT
nodes/authentication servers.  The authentication server can be a parent or
a non-parent node, which may be \emph{decoupled}, preventing newly joined
child nodes from overwhelming their parent with biased authentication load.

IoT nodes are assumed to be embedded with a PUF and are capable of performing
various cryptographic operations, such as encryption/decryption, XOR,
hashing, bitwise AND, and concatenation.

Nodes $D$ and $AS$ are assumed to be the newly joined node and its
authentication server, respectively.  $GW$ represents the gateway
symbolically.  $AS$ may be located at a singlehop or multihop distance from
$D$.  It is assumed that $GW$ has already selected the authentication server
set and has established a shared secret key with each member of the set.
Let $\mathcal{AS}' = \{AS_1, AS_2, \ldots, AS_n\}$ be the set of
authentication servers selected by $GW$, such that $AS \in \mathcal{AS}'$.
$GW$ has a shared secret key $K_{GW\text{-}AS}$ with $AS$.  It is also
assumed that $D$, whose distinct identifier is $ID_D$, possesses the address
of its authentication server $AS$ along with the authentication server's unique identity $ID_{AS}$.
Table~\ref{tab:notations} elaborates symbols and functions incorporated in
the following scheme.

There are mainly three phases in the proposed authentication scheme: node enrolment, node authentication, and node
session key exchange.  The following sections elaborate on these phases.

\subsection{Node Enrolment Phase}

The node enrolment phase is the first phase undergone by a newly joined node $D$
with its authentication server $AS$.  Before entering this phase, it is assumed
that $D$ has the required information regarding $AS$: the address and unique
identity ($ID_{AS}$) of $AS$.  $AS$ can be located at a single-hop or multi-hop
distance from $D$.  This phase is assumed to be executed in a secure environment
by establishing an end-to-end secure encrypted channel between $D$ and $AS$.  Every
newly joined node executes this phase once until it becomes disconnected from the network.
The main purpose of this phase is to enrol the unique secret of $D$ (i.e.,
$y_D$) with the accumulated value ($T_\mathsf{Acc}$) of $AS$.  During the node
authentication phase, $D$ uses $y_D$ again to get authenticated by $AS$.
PUF responses and a session-based random are also communicated between $AS$ and
$D$ in this phase to establish the secure communication of $y_D$ during
authentication process.  Figure~\ref{fig:enrolment} outlines the methodology of this
phase.

\begin{figure}[!t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.9\columnwidth]{fig_enrollment}
  \caption{Node Enrolment Phase (via secure channel.)}
  \label{fig:enrolment}
\end{figure}

$D$ initiates the phase by sending a registration request along with its unique
identity $ID_D$ to $AS$.  As a response, $AS$ generates a challenge $c_D$ from
the predefined challenge set $\mathbb{C}_D$ and a session-based random $m_D$, which are
then forwarded securely to $D$.  Both of these credentials act to
secure the communication during authentication.  $c_D$ enables $D$ to regenerate
its PUF response, while $m_D$ introduces unpredictability into subsequent
open-channel messages, preventing adversarial prediction.  With $c_D$ as input
to its embedded $\mathsf{PUF}_D$, $D$ obtains a unique PUF response $R_D$.  After this, $D$ generates its own unique secret
value $y_D$, which is also a random number.  The security of the entire scheme
critically depends on maintaining the confidentiality of $y_D$.  $D$ also picks
a reverse challenge $c_{AS\text{-}D}$ from the predefined challenge set
$\mathbb{C}_{AS\text{-}D}$ and sends $(y_D,\, R_D,\, c_{AS\text{-}D})$ back to
$AS$ through the secure channel, maintaining the confidentiality of $y_D$.
$D$ then stores $\{y_D,\, c_D,\, m_{\mathrm{curr}}\}$.  Unlike DAuth~\cite{das2026comsnets},
which stores $m_D$ as a plain session random, the proposed scheme retains it as
the initial \emph{current} session random $m_{\mathrm{curr}}$, seeding the
pseudonym mechanism described below.

$AS$ hashes the secret $y_D$ using $H(\cdot)$, generating a tag
value $Y_D^H$.  After that, $AS$ performs a bitwise AND operation
between the calculated tag $Y_D^H$ and the present accumulated value $T_\mathsf{Acc}$,
updating $T_\mathsf{Acc}$ with the result.
The accumulated value contains the compact, hashed, unique secrets of individual
nodes authenticated by $AS$.  In this way, $D$ successfully enrols itself with
$AS$.  Further, $AS$ computes its own PUF response $R_{AS\text{-}D}$
by evaluating $\mathsf{PUF}_{AS}$ on the received challenge $c_{AS\text{-}D}$, and calculates
$\Phi_{AS\text{-}D} = R_{AS\text{-}D} \oplus R_D$ by operating XOR between
$R_{AS\text{-}D}$ and the received $R_D$.  In this way, $R_D$ is kept in XORed
form for further secure communications by $AS$.  At the end, $AS$ creates an
entry against $ID_D$ and stores the calculated $\Phi_{AS\text{-}D}$ along with
the challenge $c_{AS\text{-}D}$ and the received session-based random $m_D$.

The proposed scheme extends this base enrolment with a pseudonym and dual-state
initialisation to eliminate $ID_D$ from all future open-channel messages.  Since
$ID_D$ is transmitted only over this secure enrolment channel and must never
appear on the open channel again, $D$ will henceforth be identified by the
rotating \emph{pseudonym} $\mathit{PID}$, computed as the hash of $ID_D$ concatenated with $m_{\mathrm{curr}}$, in all
future authentication messages.  Because $H$ is one-way and $m_{\mathrm{curr}}$
is a private session random, successive pseudonyms are computationally unlinkable
by a channel observer.  $AS$ pre-computes
$\mathit{PID}_{\mathrm{curr}}$ as the hash of $ID_D$ concatenated with $m_{\mathrm{curr}}$, and sets
$\mathit{PID}_{\mathrm{old}} = \varepsilon$, $m_{\mathrm{old}} = \varepsilon$, forming the
initial \emph{dual state}.  This dual state
($m_{\mathrm{curr}}$, $m_{\mathrm{old}}$, $\mathit{PID}_{\mathrm{curr}}$,
$\mathit{PID}_{\mathrm{old}}$) is maintained to handle desynchronisation.  If the
updated $m_{\mathrm{new}}$, delivered in the Key Exchange Phase, is lost in
transit, $AS$ can still recognise $D$ by its previous pseudonym and restore
synchronisation without requiring re-enrolment.  $AS$ therefore stores
$\{T_\mathsf{Acc},\, ID_D,\, \Phi_{AS\text{-}D},\, c_{AS\text{-}D},\,
m_{\mathrm{curr}},\, m_{\mathrm{old}},\, \mathit{PID}_{\mathrm{curr}},\,
\mathit{PID}_{\mathrm{old}}\}$.

\subsection{Node Authentication Phase}

The node authentication phase is the second phase of the proposed scheme.
Its main purpose is to authenticate $D$'s unique secret $y_D$ with
$AS$.  Since this phase is executed over an open channel, $y_D$ must be
communicated in secure form, preventing it from being intercepted by adversaries.
This security is established through PUF-based challenge-response, producing a
secret key $R_D$, and the session-based random $m_{\mathrm{curr}}$
incorporating unpredictability.  Both $R_D$ and $m_{\mathrm{curr}}$ are known
only to $D$ and $AS$.  Figure~\ref{fig:auth} illustrates the phase.

\begin{figure}[!t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.9\columnwidth]{fig_auth.jpg}
  \caption{Node Authentication Phase}
  \label{fig:auth}
\end{figure}

The phase is initiated by $D$.  $D$ regenerates its PUF response $R_D$
by applying the stored challenge $c_D$ to $\mathsf{PUF}_D$.  $D$ then computes
its current pseudonym $\mathit{PID}$ by hashing $ID_D$ concatenated with $m_{\mathrm{curr}}$, which is
used in place of the real identity $ID_D$ in the outgoing message, unlike DAuth~\cite{das2026comsnets}, which sends $ID_D$ directly, enabling
cross-session tracking.  $D$ produces the hashed secret $Y_D^H$ in
XORed form as
$Y_{AS\text{-}D}^H = H(y_D) \oplus H(R_D \| m_{\mathrm{curr}} \| \mathit{PID} \| ts_1)$.
The XOR mask is the hash of $R_D$ regenerated from challenge $c_D$, the
session-based random $m_{\mathrm{curr}}$, the pseudonym $\mathit{PID}$, and the
current timestamp $ts_1$.  The timestamp is used to provide replay protection to
the XORed value $Y_{AS\text{-}D}^H$.  The complete message
$(\mathit{PID},\, Y_{AS\text{-}D}^H,\, ts_1)$ is then sent to $AS$.  An
attacker will not be able to obtain $H(y_D)$ from $Y_{AS\text{-}D}^H$ without
knowing $R_D$ (which can only be generated by $D$'s PUF) and $m_{\mathrm{curr}}$
(known only to $D$ and $AS$ through enrolment).

After receiving the message, $AS$ checks for the existence of an entry matching
the received $\mathit{PID}$.  In a departure from DAuth~\cite{das2026comsnets}, $AS$ performs
a dual-state lookup, checking whether the received $\mathit{PID}$ matches
$\mathit{PID}_{\mathrm{curr}}$ or $\mathit{PID}_{\mathrm{old}}$ in its records.
A match on $\mathit{PID}_{\mathrm{old}}$ indicates that $D$ missed the Key
Exchange Phase delivery in the previous session and still holds the prior
$m_{\mathrm{curr}}$. In this case, $AS$ uses the stored $m_{\mathrm{old}}$ for
the subsequent verification, recovering from the desynchronisation without
requiring re-enrolment.  $AS$ verifies the freshness of the message using $ts_1$.  $R_D'$ is
calculated as $\Phi_{AS\text{-}D} \oplus \mathsf{PUF}_{AS}(c_{AS\text{-}D})$,
where both $\Phi_{AS\text{-}D}$ and $c_{AS\text{-}D}$ are retrieved from $D$'s
enrolment record.  $AS$ regenerates the same XOR mask and unmasks $Y_D^{H'}$,
then performs the bitwise AND check by computing
$T_\mathsf{Acc} \mathbin{\&} Y_D^{H'}$ and verifying that it equals $T_\mathsf{Acc}$.
From the properties of the hash-based one-way accumulator, if an element is
combined into an accumulated value using bitwise AND and the same operation is
again performed between them, it gives back the same accumulated value.  If
$T_\mathsf{Acc} \mathbin{\&} Y_D^{H'}$ equals $T_\mathsf{Acc}$, it proves that the unique
secret of $D$ is already a legitimate member of $AS$.  With the successful
operation, the authentication of $D$ is completed by $AS$, and $AS$ proceeds
to the Key Exchange Phase.

\subsection{Node Session Key Exchange Phase}

This phase is executed by $AS$ to inform $GW$ about the authentication of the
newly joined node $D$ at the end of the node authentication phase.  $AS$ uses
an $\mathsf{auth\_token}_D$ to deliver the session key $K_{GW\text{-}D}$ to
$GW$, facilitating session key generation between $GW$ and $D$.  The proposed
scheme extends DAuth~\cite{das2026comsnets} by additionally deriving
a fresh pseudonym $\mathit{PID}_{\mathrm{new}} = H(ID_D \| m_{\mathrm{new}})$
at the end of each session and carrying it within $\mathsf{auth\_token}_D$, so
that $GW$ can update its pseudonym mapping for $D$ and successive sessions
remain unlinkable.  Figure~\ref{fig:keyex} shows the phase.

$K_{GW\text{-}D}$ is calculated by $AS$ by hashing the concatenation of $R_D'$ and
$m_{\mathrm{new}}$.
$R_D'$ is the PUF response of $D$ regenerated by $AS$ during the node
authentication phase, and thus known only to $AS$ and $D$.  $m_{\mathrm{new}}$,
on the other hand, is computed as the hash of a fresh random number $n_1$ generated
by $AS$.  The computed $m_{\mathrm{new}}$ will
also act as the updated session-based random for the next authentication session
between $AS$ and $D$, incorporating an update to the authentication secret.
Further, $AS$ derives $\mathit{PID}_{\mathrm{new}}$ by hashing the concatenation of $ID_D$ and $m_{\mathrm{new}}$.
Because $\mathit{PID}_{\mathrm{new}}$ is based on the fresh $m_{\mathrm{new}}$,
it is computationally unlinkable to the pseudonym used in the current session.
The session key $K_{GW\text{-}D}$, the identities $ID_{AS}$ and $ID_D$, the
next pseudonym $\mathit{PID}_{\mathrm{new}}$, and the authentication completion
timestamp $ts_{\mathrm{auth}}$ are encrypted under the shared key $K_{GW\text{-}AS}$
(retrieved from storage) to form $\mathsf{auth\_token}_D$.  $AS$ simultaneously
sends $(m^H,\, ts_2)$ to $D$ and $(\mathit{PID}_{\mathrm{new}},\, ID_{AS},\, \mathsf{auth\_token}_D)$ to $GW$.
It then saves the current nonce and pseudonym as the previous values and advances
its records by setting $m_{\mathrm{curr}}$ to $m_{\mathrm{new}}$
and $\mathit{PID}_{\mathrm{curr}}$ to $\mathit{PID}_{\mathrm{new}}$, while
$m_{\mathrm{old}}$ and $\mathit{PID}_{\mathrm{old}}$ retain the superseded values.

On the other hand, $AS$ also informs $D$ of $m_{\mathrm{new}}$ so that $D$
can independently compute the session key $K_{GW\text{-}D}$ and its next
pseudonym.  Since $m_{\mathrm{new}}$ must be protected from adversaries (its
capture would allow prediction of future session key material), $AS$ masks it as
$m^H = m_{\mathrm{new}} \oplus H(Y_D^{H'} \| m_{\mathrm{curr}} \| R_D' \| ID_{AS} \| \mathit{PID}_{\mathrm{curr}} \| ts_2)$.
Here, $Y_D^{H'}$ is the hashed form of the unique secret of $D$, known to $AS$
from the authentication phase.  Both $m_{\mathrm{curr}}$ and $R_D'$ are likewise
known to $AS$ from the authentication phase.  The current timestamp $ts_2$ is
used to prevent replay of $m^H$ in a future session.

Obtaining $(m^H,\, ts_2)$, $D$ first verifies the freshness of $ts_2$.  $D$
then recovers $m_{\mathrm{new}}$ by regenerating the hash mask using its locally
held $Y_D^H$, $R_D$, $m_{\mathrm{curr}}$, $ID_{AS}$, $\mathit{PID}_{\mathrm{curr}}$, and the
received $ts_2$, and XORing with $m^H$.  The input of the regenerated hash is
already known by $D$.  In this way, $D$ and $AS$ update their shared
session-based random $m_{\mathrm{curr}}$ with $m_{\mathrm{new}}$.  Alongside,
$D$ establishes the session key $K_{GW\text{-}D}$ as the hash of the concatenation of $R_D$ and $m_{\mathrm{new}}$,
and rotates its pseudonym $\mathit{PID}_{\mathrm{curr}}$ by hashing $ID_D$ with $m_{\mathrm{new}}$,
thus preparing itself for secure communication with $GW$.

$GW$ decrypts the token with $K_{GW\text{-}AS}$, verifies $ts_{\mathrm{auth}}$,
and extracts $K_{GW\text{-}D}$ and $\mathit{PID}_{\mathrm{new}}$.  $GW$ updates
its stored pseudonym for $D$ to $\mathit{PID}_{\mathrm{new}}$ and associates the
session key $K_{GW\text{-}D}$ with it, so that $D$'s subsequent data frames
carrying $\mathit{PID}_{\mathrm{new}}$ are recognised and served.

\subsection{Desynchronisation Recovery}

\begin{figure}[!t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.9\columnwidth]{fig_keyex}
  \caption{Node Session Key Exchange Phase}
  \label{fig:keyex}
\end{figure}

During the Key Exchange Phase, $AS$ simultaneously dispatches two packets:
$(\mathit{PID}_{\mathrm{new}},\, ID_{AS},\, \mathsf{auth\_token}_D)$ to $GW$
and $(m^H,\, ts_2)$ to $D$.
Loss of either packet leads to a different desynchronisation scenario, each
handled without re-enrolment.

If the packet from $AS$ to $GW$ is lost, $D$ has already received $(m^H,\, ts_2)$
and updated its state to $\mathit{PID}_{\mathrm{new}}$ and $K_{GW\text{-}D}$.
$GW$, however, never received $\mathit{PID}_{\mathrm{new}}$ or the session key,
so it rejects $D$'s subsequent data frames.
$D$ detects the failure and re-initiates the Authentication Phase with its
current $\mathit{PID}_{\mathrm{curr}}$.
Since $D$ and $AS$ are already synchronised, $AS$ recognises $\mathit{PID}_{\mathrm{curr}}$
on the normal path, completes authentication, and forwards a fresh
$\mathsf{auth\_token}_D$ to $GW$, restoring normal operation in one additional round.

The primary desynchronisation scenario arises when the packet from $AS$ to $D$
$(m^H,\, ts_2)$ is lost in transit.  $AS$ has already advanced its internal
state by updating $m_{\mathrm{curr}}$ to $m_{\mathrm{new}}$ and rotating
$\mathit{PID}_{\mathrm{curr}}$ to $\mathit{PID}_{\mathrm{new}}$, while $D$
retains the stale $m_{\mathrm{curr}}$.  On the next authentication attempt,
$D$ computes its pseudonym from the stale nonce, producing a $\mathit{PID}$
that no longer matches any record at $AS$.  The XOR mask used to protect
$Y_{AS\text{-}D}^H$ is likewise constructed from the stale $m_{\mathrm{curr}}$,
so $AS$ cannot reconstruct the correct mask and the accumulator check fails.
Authentication is therefore rejected.  In schemes without a recovery mechanism,
this failure forces $D$ to execute the full enrolment phase again, a costly
operation for a resource-constrained node.

The proposed dual-state mechanism avoids this re-enrolment cost.  If the Key
Exchange Phase delivery $(m^H,\,ts_2)$ is lost, $D$ retains its stale
$m_{\mathrm{curr}}$, so the pseudonym it computes in the next session equals
$\mathit{PID}_{\mathrm{old}}$ from $AS$'s perspective.  Algorithms~\ref{alg:desync-auth} and~\ref{alg:desync-keyex}
show how the scheme adapts the Authentication and Key Exchange Phases to recover without re-enrolment.
$D$'s behaviour is \emph{identical} in both paths, as it always uses its own
$m_{\mathrm{curr}}$ and $\mathit{PID}$.  Only $AS$ adapts.  The dual-state
lookup detects which nonce $D$ is on, then constructs $m^H$ with the matching
$(m_{\mathrm{old}},\,\mathit{PID}_{\mathrm{old}})$ so that $D$'s symmetric
decryption succeeds and the states converge.
The dual-state mechanism tolerates a single consecutive Key Exchange Phase loss, but
two or more consecutive losses exhaust both slots, requiring re-enrolment, a
deliberate trade-off between storage overhead and recovery depth.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Desynchronisation Recovery -- Authentication Phase}
\label{alg:desync-auth}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Statex \textbf{Precondition:} $D$ holds stale $m_{\mathrm{curr}} = m_{\mathrm{old}}$;\;
       $AS$ holds $(m_{\mathrm{curr}},\,m_{\mathrm{old}},\,
       \mathit{PID}_{\mathrm{curr}},\,\mathit{PID}_{\mathrm{old}})$
\State $\mathit{PID} = H(\mathit{ID}_D \| m_{\mathrm{curr}})$
\State $D$ sends $(\mathit{PID},\; Y_{AS\text{-}D}^H,\; ts_1)$ to $AS$
\State AS verifies freshness of $ts_1$
\State AS calculates $R_D' = \Phi_{AS\text{-}D} \oplus \mathsf{PUF}_{AS}(c_{AS\text{-}D})$
\If{$\mathit{PID} == \mathit{PID}_{\mathrm{old}}$}
    \State $Y_D^{H'} = Y_{AS\text{-}D}^H \oplus
           H(R_D' \| m_{\mathrm{old}} \| \mathit{PID} \| ts_1)$
\EndIf
\If{$\mathit{PID} == \mathit{PID}_{\mathrm{curr}}$}
    \State $Y_D^{H'} = Y_{AS\text{-}D}^H \oplus
           H(R_D' \| m_{\mathrm{curr}} \| \mathit{PID} \| ts_1)$
\EndIf
\If{$T_\mathsf{Acc} \mathbin{\&} Y_D^{H'} == T_\mathsf{Acc}$}
    \State Node $D$ authenticated
\Else
    \State Not authenticated
\EndIf
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Desynchronisation Recovery -- Key Exchange Phase}
\label{alg:desync-keyex}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Statex \textbf{Precondition:} desync path from Algorithm~\ref{alg:desync-auth};\;
       $AS$ uses $m_{\mathrm{old}}$, $\mathit{PID}_{\mathrm{old}}$
\State $AS$ generates random value $n_1$
\State $m_{\mathrm{new}} = H(n_1)$
\State $m^H = m_{\mathrm{new}} \oplus
       H\!\left(Y_D^{H'} \| m_{\mathrm{old}} \| R_D' \|
       \mathit{ID}_{AS} \| \mathit{PID}_{\mathrm{old}} \| ts_2\right)$
\State $AS$ sends $(m^H,\; ts_2)$ to $D$
\State $m_{\mathrm{new}} = m^H \oplus
       H\!\left(Y_D^{H} \| m_{\mathrm{curr}} \| R_D \|
       \mathit{ID}_{AS} \| \mathit{PID} \| ts_2\right)$
       
\State $m_{\mathrm{old}} = m_{\mathrm{curr}}$,\quad
       $m_{\mathrm{curr}} = m_{\mathrm{new}}$,\quad
       $\mathit{PID} = H(\mathit{ID}_D \| m_{\mathrm{new}})$
\Statex \textbf{Result:} $D$ and $AS$ re-synchronised, $\mathit{PID}$ is rotated.
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\section{Security Analysis}

\subsection{Formal Security Analysis}

The proposed scheme is translated into ProVerif~\cite{blanchet2018proverif}
queries under the Dolev--Yao adversary model~\cite{dolev1983security}, where
the adversary has full control of the public channel and can intercept, replay,
modify, and inject messages.  Ten queries are verified and all are satisfied.
Figure~\ref{fig:proverif} shows the verification summary.

\begin{figure*}[!t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig_proverif}
  \caption{ProVerif Output.}
  \label{fig:proverif}
\end{figure*}

\subsubsection{Protocol Correspondence}

Five correspondence queries verify that the protocol steps happen in the correct
order and that no adversary can skip or forge a step.

The first query checks enrolment integrity.  A device can only be registered at
the authentication server after it has explicitly initiated the enrolment
process.  This rules out any possibility of a device being enrolled without
its own participation.

The second query checks the device side of the authentication phase.  The device
receives the server's acknowledgement and the fresh timestamp $ts_2$ only after
it first sent a valid authentication request.  This prevents an adversary from
replaying a stale acknowledgement to trick the device into advancing its state.

The third query checks the server side of the authentication phase.  The authentication
server completes its processing only after it received a structurally correct
request from the device.  A forged or replayed message cannot push the server
into a completed state.

The fourth query binds the Authentication and Key Exchange phases.  The PUF response $R_D$ and
the session seed $m_{\mathrm{curr}}$ that the device commits during authentication are proven to be
exactly the same values used in the key exchange.  This rules out an active
adversary who might try to substitute credentials between phases.

The fifth query covers key exchange.  The device recovers the new session seed
$m_{\mathrm{new}}$ only after it legitimately sent the key-exchange request.
No key material can be delivered to the device out of sequence.

\subsubsection{Secrecy and Forward Secrecy}

Two queries verify the secrecy of the session key
$K_{GW\text{-}D} = H(R_D \| m_{\mathrm{new}})$ from the perspective of the
device and the gateway independently.  Even though the adversary observes all
traffic on the public channel, it cannot reconstruct $K_{GW\text{-}D}$
because the PUF response $R_D$ is never transmitted and
$m_{\mathrm{new}}$ is always delivered XOR-masked.

A separate query confirms forward secrecy, proving that the rotated session seed
$m_{\mathrm{new}}$ itself remains secret.  This means that even if an
adversary somehow recovers a current session key, it gains nothing about
future sessions, because each $m_{\mathrm{new}}$ is derived from an
independently generated fresh nonce.

\subsubsection{Anonymity Preservation}

The most significant secrecy result concerns the real device identity $ID_D$.
After enrolment, $ID_D$ is never sent over the open channel again.  Every
subsequent message instead carries the pseudonym
$\mathit{PID} = H(ID_D \| m_{\mathrm{curr}})$.
ProVerif formally confirms that no attacker, even one who intercepts and manipulates every single
message on the network, can recover $ID_D$ from the observed traffic.

This result supports device anonymity.  However, anonymity is only meaningful
if the pseudonym used in one session cannot be linked to the pseudonym used in
the next.  The forward-secrecy query addresses this.  Because
$m_{\mathrm{new}}$ is proven secret, consecutive pseudonyms
$H(ID_D \| m_{\mathrm{curr}})$ and $H(ID_D \| m_{\mathrm{new}})$ are
computationally indistinguishable to any observer, making it difficult for an
adversary who collects pseudonyms from multiple sessions to determine whether
they belong to the same physical device.

\subsubsection{Offline Guessing Resistance}

Finally, a weak-secrecy query confirms that the session key $K_{GW\text{-}D}$
cannot be guessed from the public transcript even by an adversary with
unlimited offline dictionary power.  This closes the gap against brute-force
and dictionary attacks that target the key directly from captured packets.

\subsection{Informal Analysis}

\subsubsection{Replay Attack Resistance}
The Enrolment Phase is executed over a secure channel and immune to replay.
The Authentication and Key Exchange Phases embed timestamps
($ts_1$, $ts_2$, $ts_{\mathrm{auth}}$) and the session-based random
$m_{\mathrm{curr}}$ into the hash masks.  A replayed message with a stale
timestamp or stale $m_{\mathrm{curr}}$ will fail the freshness check at
$AS$, $D$, or $GW$ respectively.

\subsubsection{MITM Attack Resistance}
The Enrolment Phase is conducted over an encrypted channel, which precludes
MITM.  In the Authentication and Key Exchange Phases, the PUF response $R_D$ (device-specific, unreproducible) and
session random $m_{\mathrm{curr}}$ (private to $D$ and $AS$) are embedded in
every XOR mask.  Without $R_D$ and the current $m_{\mathrm{curr}}$, no
adversary can forge a valid masked tag $Y_{AS\text{-}D}^H$ or unmask $m^H$.

\subsubsection{Device Anonymity}
$ID_D$ is transmitted only once, over the secure enrolment channel, and is
never exposed on the open network again.  All open-channel messages use
$\mathit{PID} = H(ID_D \| m_{\mathrm{curr}})$, which is refreshed to
$H(ID_D \| m_{\mathrm{new}})$ after every key exchange.  Because $m_{\mathrm{new}}$
is derived from a freshly generated nonce $n_1$, successive pseudonyms are
computationally unlinkable.

\subsubsection{Desynchronisation Recovery}
Packet loss can occur at three distinct points across the Authentication and Key
Exchange Phases, all handled without re-enrolment.  Of these, only the loss of the
Key Exchange response causes a genuine state divergence between $D$ and $AS$.  The
other two are resolved by simple retransmission or routine re-authentication.

If the acknowledgement and fresh timestamp $ts_2$ sent by $AS$ to $D$ at the
end of the authentication phase are lost in transit, $D$ does not advance its internal state
and retains $m_{\mathrm{curr}}$ and the current $\mathit{PID}$ unchanged.
Since $AS$ has not yet performed any state update at this stage, both
entities remain consistent.  Upon timeout, $D$ retransmits the authentication request
with the same $\mathit{PID}$ and $ts_1$, and the exchange proceeds
normally.

The primary desynchronisation scenario arises when the Key Exchange Phase response
carrying the masked session seed $m^H$, sent from $AS$ to $D$, is lost.  In this
case, $AS$ has already advanced its state to $m_{\mathrm{new}}$ and rotated
the pseudonym to $\mathit{PID}_{\mathrm{new}}$, while $D$ retains the
previous $m_{\mathrm{curr}}$.  On the subsequent authentication attempt, $D$
constructs its pseudonym from the stale $m_{\mathrm{curr}}$, producing
$\mathit{PID}_{\mathrm{old}}$.  $AS$ recognises this via the dual-state
lookup --- storing both $(m_{\mathrm{curr}}, \mathit{PID}_{\mathrm{curr}})$ and
$(m_{\mathrm{old}}, \mathit{PID}_{\mathrm{old}})$ --- and resumes from the
consistent prior state, restoring synchronisation without any re-enrolment
overhead.

If the token forwarded from $AS$ to $GW$ is lost, $GW$ does not receive
$\mathit{PID}_{\mathrm{new}}$ or the associated session key $K_{GW\text{-}D}$.
Consequently, $D$'s subsequent data packet, carrying $\mathit{PID}_{\mathrm{new}}$,
is not recognised by $GW$.  $D$ re-initiates from the Authentication Phase, and the dual-state
storage at $AS$ ensures that this re-authentication succeeds and a fresh
token is forwarded to $GW$, restoring normal operation.

\subsubsection{Forward Secrecy}
$K_{GW\text{-}D} = H(R_D \| m_{\mathrm{new}})$.  Compromise of a session key
does not reveal previous session keys because $m_{\mathrm{new}}$ is
independently generated from a fresh nonce and $R_D$ is PUF-derived.

\subsection{Theoretical Performance Analysis}
\subsubsection{Computational Cost}

The computational cost is a crucial deterministic factor for the efficiency of an
authentication scheme. To obtain platform-native unit costs consistent with our hardware
evaluation, we adopt the per-primitive execution times reported by Lee et
al.~\cite{lee2025plakamd}, measured on a Raspberry Pi~4B (quad-core 64-bit ARM Cortex-A72
@ 1.5\,GHz) using the MIRACL Core cryptographic library. The reference unit costs are:
SHA-256 hash $T_{\mathsf{hash}}=0.009$\,ms, AES-128 encryption/decryption
$T_{\mathsf{aes}}=0.004$\,ms, PUF evaluation $T_{\mathsf{puf}}=0.12$\,ms, and fuzzy
extractor $T_{\mathsf{fe}}=2.353$\,ms. Following the standard code-offset
construction~\cite{dodis2004fuzzy}, the dominant cost of the fuzzy extractor is one
elliptic-curve scalar multiplication, hence $T_{\mathsf{fe}}=T_{\mathsf{ecc}}$. The cost of
random-number generation and bitwise operations (XOR, concatenation) is negligible
relative to these primitives and is therefore omitted, consistent with prior
analyses~\cite{zhou2024auth}.

The proposed scheme relies exclusively on PUF, hash, and AES operations, and employs no
public-key or fuzzy-extractor primitive.

During node enrolment (Figure~\ref{fig:enrolment}), $D$ and $AS$ each evaluate the PUF
once and two hash evaluations are performed, giving
$2T_{\mathsf{puf}}+2T_{\mathsf{hash}}=0.258$\,ms. As shown in
Table~\ref{tab:comp_enroll}, this is marginally above DAuth~\cite{das2026comsnets}
($0.249$\,ms), the difference being exactly one additional hash evaluation.
Zhou et al.~\cite{zhou2024auth} incurs the highest enrolment cost
($2.491$\,ms), dominated by the fuzzy-extractor generation function $\mathsf{Gen}(\cdot)$
($T_{\mathsf{fe}}=T_{\mathsf{ecc}}=2.353$\,ms).

\subsubsection{Communication Cost}

Communication cost is a core feature for analysing the performance of an authentication
scheme, based on the number of message exchanges and their sizes (in bits). By assuming
that communications are executed with uniform bit lengths, the communication cost of both
enrolment and the authentication and key exchange phase is evaluated and compared with
other state-of-the-art schemes.

It is assumed that identities and timestamps are 32\,bits, along with nonces, hashes,
challenges, responses, and ciphertexts as 256\,bits, ensuring a fair comparison.

During the node enrolment phase, $D$ enrols itself with $AS$, executing three message
exchanges. The first message communicates the unique identity of $D$, i.e., $ID_D$
(32\,bits), to $AS$ over the secure channel. In the second message, $AS$ transmits a
challenge $c_D$ (256\,bits) and a session-based random $m_D$ (256\,bits) to $D$. The
third communication takes place from $D$ to $AS$ in the form of a unique secret $y_D$
(256\,bits), a PUF response $R_D$ (256\,bits), and a reverse challenge
$c_{AS\text{-}D}$ (256\,bits). Thus, the total communication cost per enrolment is
1312\,bits as presented in Table~\ref{tab:comm_enroll}. It outperforms
LAAKA~\cite{ali2024laaka} (2592\,bits) and Zhou et al.~\cite{zhou2024auth}
(1344\,bits), and matches DAuth~\cite{das2026comsnets} (1312\,bits) exactly, demonstrating
that pseudonym initialisation adds no communication overhead over the base scheme. Unlike
LAAKA and Zhou, the proposed scheme binds device enrolment to a PUF-derived secret and
initialises the pseudonym at registration, adding security guarantees without increasing
communication cost.

\subsubsection{Storage Cost}

The storage cost captures the persistent credentials each entity must retain to
run authentication after enrolment, which is a decisive constraint for
memory-limited IoT endpoints. To enable a consistent cross-scheme comparison, the
storage set declared by each scheme is taken from its own specification, and all
cryptographic values---hashes, nonces, challenges, PUF responses, ciphertexts,
symmetric keys, and rotating pseudonymous identifiers---are normalised to
256\,bits, while fixed real identities are taken as 32\,bits, in line with the
assumptions adopted for the communication-cost analysis. For Zhou
et al.~\cite{zhou2024auth}, the elliptic-curve--derived dynamic identity and the
PUF challenge--response values are likewise counted at 256\,bits each. The
per-entity footprints obtained under these assumptions are summarised in
Table~\ref{tab:storage}.

In LAAKA~\cite{ali2024laaka}, following the scheme's own storage accounting
(Table~6 of~\cite{ali2024laaka}, re-normalised here to the common 256-bit unit),
the IoT device keeps its temporary identity $TID_d$, the fog temporary identity
$TID_f$, the masked secret $A_f$, and the shared anchor $B_k$ (1024\,bits). The fog
server (FS) authenticates devices locally and stores $TID_d$, $TID_f$, $A_d$, and
$B_k$ (1024\,bits). The registration authority (RA) is the root of trust and
retains $TID_d$, $TID_f$, $A_d$, $A_f$, $B_k$, and the long-term master key $K$
(1536\,bits); it never stores the real identity $ID_d$, as the device registers by
sending only $A_d = h(ID_d \| r_2)$.

In Zhou et al.~\cite{zhou2024auth}, the gateway acts as the combined authenticator
and router. Per device it maintains the secret key $k_i$, the identity $ID_i$, the
dynamic identity $DID_i$, the sensor identity $SID_n$, the real sensor identity
$SN_n$ (used to refresh $SID_n^{\mathrm{new}} = SN_n \oplus b_n^{\mathrm{new}}$),
and the PUF challenge--response pair $(C_n, R_n)$, totalling 1344\,bits. The user
device holds its identity $ID_i$, the masked credential $CPW_i$, the helper string
$hid_i$, $DID_i$, and $SID_n$ (1056\,bits); the secret $k_i$ is regenerated from
$hid_i$ through the fuzzy extractor rather than stored. The sensor node, serving
only as the data endpoint, stores the single identifier $SID_n$ (256\,bits).

In DAuth~\cite{das2026comsnets}, the PUF-based device avoids long-term key storage
yet still persists its unique secret $y_D$, the challenge $c_D$, and the current
session nonce $m_{\mathrm{curr}}$ (768\,bits); the PUF response $R_D$ is regenerated
on demand from $c_D$, but $y_D$---the secret enrolled with the accumulator---is
stored. The gateway-selected AS validates the device through a one-way accumulator
and retains the accumulated value $T_{\mathsf{Acc}}$, the identity $ID_D$, the
PUF-binding $\Phi_{AS\text{-}D}$, the reverse challenge $c_{AS\text{-}D}$, and
$m_{\mathrm{curr}}$ (1056\,bits), while the gateway, as the secure-channel endpoint,
keeps the session key $K_{GW\text{-}D}$ and $ID_D$ (288\,bits).

The proposed scheme inherits the same device footprint as DAuth (768\,bits),
because the rotating pseudonym $\mathit{PID} = H(ID_D \| m_{\mathrm{curr}})$ is
derived on-the-fly rather than stored. To support single-loss desynchronisation
recovery, the AS additionally retains the previous nonce $m_{\mathrm{old}}$ and the
dual pseudonym pair $(\mathit{PID}_{\mathrm{curr}}, \mathit{PID}_{\mathrm{old}})$ on
top of the DAuth state, raising its footprint to 1824\,bits. Because the real
identity is never exposed on the open channel, the gateway substitutes
$\mathit{PID}_{\mathrm{curr}}$ for $ID_D$ and stores it alongside $K_{GW\text{-}D}$
(512\,bits).

As shown in Table~\ref{tab:storage}, the proposed scheme and DAuth attain the
lowest device-side storage (768\,bits), roughly 25\,\% below LAAKA (1024\,bits)
and 27\,\% below Zhou et al.\ (1056\,bits), making them well-suited for
memory-constrained IoT endpoints where minimising resource usage is crucial. The
modest additional state introduced for anonymity and desynchronisation recovery is
confined to the AS, a gateway-selected node with ample resources, and therefore
imposes no burden on the constrained device.

Simulation-based performance analysis is important as it evaluates the proposed
authentication scheme by considering the impact of message communication on performance
metrics, such as energy and CPU time, in a multihop network. A comprehensive
simulation-based performance analysis is performed, assessing the performance efficiency
of the proposed scheme against DAuth~\cite{das2026comsnets}, LAAKA~\cite{ali2024laaka},
and Zhou et al.~\cite{zhou2024auth}. The simulation is executed in the COOJA simulator
using Cooja mote inside the Contiki-NG environment, where the CoAP protocol is utilized
for the communication. A 100-node network is randomly deployed as the simulation
scenario. The simulation is repeated ten times with random seeds. The propagation model in the simulation

\subsubsection{Performance Analysis for Desynchronisation Recovery}
\label{sec:desync_perf}

Desynchronisation affects protocols that rotate session state after each
successful authentication.
It arises when the packet delivering the updated state to one party is lost,
leaving the sender on the new state while the receiver retains the old one.
The next authentication attempt then fails from this mismatch, and the cost of
recovery depends on what fallback mechanism the scheme provides.

In DAuth~\cite{das2026comsnets}, desynchronisation is triggered when the
key-delivery packet $(m^H,\, ts_2)$ sent to the device at the end of the Key
Exchange Phase is lost.
The authentication server has already advanced its nonce to $m_{\mathrm{new}}$
while the device retains $m_{\mathrm{curr}}$.
As DAuth stores only a single nonce state, the device's next attempt uses the
stale $m_{\mathrm{curr}}$ and produces a pseudonym the server cannot recognise,
so the request is rejected.
Recovery then demands a complete re-enrolment, re-executing the PUF
challenge-response exchange and reconstructing the hash-accumulator-based
group-membership credential before authentication can resume.

The proposed scheme shares the same protocol structure as DAuth and is exposed
to the identical trigger, but differs in its server state model.
The authentication server retains a dual state,
$(m_{\mathrm{curr}},\,\mathit{PID}_{\mathrm{curr}})$ and
$(m_{\mathrm{old}},\,\mathit{PID}_{\mathrm{old}})$.
When the device re-authenticates with the stale pseudonym
$\mathit{PID}_{\mathrm{old}}$, the server recognises it via the
$\mathit{PID}_{\mathrm{old}}$ lookup and runs the full authentication protocol
against the old state, recovering $R_D$, deriving $Y_{AS\text{-}D}^H$,
verifying the accumulator against $m_{\mathrm{old}}$, and completing a fresh
key exchange without re-enrolment.

Zhou et al.\ also rotate their pseudonyms, but the gateway \emph{adds} each
device's new pseudonym $\mathit{DID}_{i,\mathrm{new}}$ to the user record while
\emph{retaining} the previous $\mathit{DID}_i$.
A device that misses the final confirmation message~(M4) is therefore still
recognised on its prior pseudonym and re-synchronised through an ordinary
authentication round, which re-delivers the current sensor pseudonym, without
re-registration.

LAAKA also rotates its temporal identifier, deriving
$\mathit{TID}_{d,\mathrm{new}} = \mathit{TID}_d \oplus r_d$ from a fresh random
$r_d$ each session, so it too is anonymity-preserving.
Its update, however, is computed entirely from randoms exchanged within the
authentication round, and the fog server retains the prior temporal identifier,
so a device that misses the final confirmation simply represents its current
$\mathit{TID}_d$ and re-authenticates without a stored-state mismatch.
Because both LAAKA and Zhou et al.\ retain the previous identifier, neither holds
a single rotating secret that can drift irreconcilably between the parties, so a
like-for-like desynchronisation-recovery scenario does not apply to either and
both are excluded.
The evaluation therefore compares DAuth and the proposed scheme only, which share the same trigger and threat model for desynchronisation but differ in their recovery mechanism.
Figure~\ref{fig:desync_bar} reports the per-device cost of a recovery round,
taken immediately after a desynchronisation event, over a 100-node RPL network
(20 devices, 5 seeds).
In DAuth~\cite{das2026comsnets}, recovery requires a failed authentication, a
full re-enrolment, and a fresh authentication and key exchange.
The proposed dual-state lookup instead recovers with a single re-authentication
and key exchange, cutting per-device recovery energy and CPU time by 35.3\,\%
over DAuth.

Hardware-based analysis validates the feasibility of the proposed scheme under
real device conditions.
The proposed scheme and the three comparison schemes, DAuth~\cite{das2026comsnets},
LAAKA~\cite{ali2024laaka}, and Zhou et al.~\cite{zhou2024auth}, are implemented
on a uniform testbed of Raspberry Pi 4B platforms (quad-core 64-bit ARM
Cortex-A72 at 1.5\,GHz), using the MIRACL Core library for all operations.
Two Raspberry Pi 4B boards act as IoT nodes, one as the newly joined device
initiating authentication and the other as the authentication server/fog node performing
verification and key exchange, while the laptop serves as the gateway handling
token delivery and session management.
The three entities communicate directly over TCP, and the physical testbed is
shown in Figure~\ref{fig:hw_setup}.

pseudonym rotation and the dual-state nonce lookup are sub-millisecond relative to
the network round-trip time and introduce no measurable overhead over the
non-anonymous baseline.
Both outperform LAAKA~\cite{ali2024laaka} by approximately 36\,\% and
Zhou et al.~\cite{zhou2024auth} by approximately 43\,\% in energy consumption.
The higher cost of Zhou et al.\ arises from a nested gateway-to-sensor-node

remaining below both LAAKA~\cite{ali2024laaka} and Zhou et al.~\cite{zhou2024auth}
across energy and execution time.
The ordering of schemes is consistent between the COOJA simulation and the hardware
evaluation, confirming that the proposed scheme's advantage reflects protocol design
rather than platform-specific effects.
The hardware evaluation validates that provable anonymity is achieved at no measurable
cost penalty over the non-anonymous baseline on real IoT devices.

\section{Conclusion}

This paper presented a lightweight, anonymous, and decoupled distributed
authentication scheme for multihop IoT networks.  The assigned
authentication server, which may or may not be the parent of the newly joined
device, performs authentication (Authentication Phase) and session key
establishment (Key Exchange Phase) in sequential phases.  Device anonymity is preserved
through per-session pseudonym rotation, preventing adversarial tracking across
sessions.  A dual-state mechanism at the authentication server provides transparent
desynchronisation recovery without re-enrolment.
PUF-based authentication eliminates long-term cryptographic key storage, and
hash-based accumulator operations with XOR masking keep the computational
overhead low.


% -------------------------------------------------------
\section{Future Work}

Reducing the dual-state storage overhead at the AS while preserving
desynchronisation resilience is a promising direction for further optimisation.
The present scheme also does not conceal the authentication server identity,
as $\mathit{ID}_{AS}$ appears in open-channel messages.
Extending pseudonym rotation to authentication servers would provide full
end-to-end identity unlinkability across the multihop network.
