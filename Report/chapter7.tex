\chapter{Performance Analysis}

\section{Theoretical Performance Analysis}

\subsection{Computational Cost}

Computational cost directly determines how efficiently an authentication scheme
can be deployed on resource-constrained hardware.
To obtain platform-native unit costs consistent with the hardware evaluation
(Section~\ref{sec:hw}), the per-primitive execution times reported by Lee et
al.~\cite{lee2025plakamd} are adopted, measured on a Raspberry Pi~4B (quad-core
64-bit ARM Cortex-A72 @ 1.5\,GHz) using the MIRACL Core cryptographic library.
The reference unit costs are: SHA-256 hash $T_{\mathsf{hash}} = 0.009$\,ms,
AES-128 encryption/decryption $T_{\mathsf{aes}} = 0.004$\,ms, PUF evaluation
$T_{\mathsf{puf}} = 0.12$\,ms, and fuzzy extractor $T_{\mathsf{fe}} = 2.353$\,ms.
Following the standard code-offset construction~\cite{dodis2004fuzzy}, the dominant
cost of the fuzzy extractor is one elliptic-curve scalar multiplication, hence
$T_{\mathsf{fe}} = T_{\mathsf{ecc}}$.
The cost of random-number generation and bitwise operations (XOR, concatenation)
is negligible relative to these primitives and is therefore omitted, consistent
with prior analyses~\cite{zhou2024auth}.
The proposed scheme relies exclusively on PUF, hash, and AES operations, and
employs no public-key or fuzzy-extractor primitive.

\subsubsection{Enrolment Phase}

During node enrolment, $D$ and $AS$ each evaluate the PUF once, and two hash
evaluations are performed (the unique-secret hash $H(y_D)$ and the pseudonym
initialisation $H(ID_D \| m_{\mathrm{curr}})$), giving
$2T_{\mathsf{puf}} + 2T_{\mathsf{hash}} = \mathbf{0.258}$\,ms.
As shown in Table~\ref{tab:comp_enroll}, this is marginally above
DAuth~\cite{das2026comsnets} (0.249\,ms), the difference being exactly one
additional hash evaluation for pseudonym initialisation, a security feature absent
from DAuth.
Zhou et al.~\cite{zhou2024auth} incurs the highest enrolment cost (2.491\,ms),
dominated by the fuzzy-extractor generation function ($T_{\mathsf{fe}} =
T_{\mathsf{ecc}} = 2.353$\,ms), whereas LAAKA~\cite{ali2024laaka} (0.027\,ms) uses
only hash operations and no PUF.

\subsubsection{Authentication and Key Exchange Phase}

In the authentication and key exchange phases, $D$ and $AS$ each evaluate the PUF
once, $D$ performs six hash evaluations, $AS$ performs five hash evaluations and one
AES encryption, and $GW$ performs one AES decryption.
This yields $2T_{\mathsf{puf}} + 11T_{\mathsf{hash}} + 2T_{\mathsf{aes}} =
\mathbf{0.347}$\,ms, as shown in Table~\ref{tab:comp_auth}.
The proposed scheme is second only to DAuth~\cite{das2026comsnets} (0.320\,ms), the
0.027\,ms overhead being attributable to three additional hash evaluations for
pseudonym computation, dual-state lookup, and pseudonym rotation.
LAAKA~\cite{ali2024laaka} (0.144\,ms) achieves lower cost by performing only hash
operations without any PUF evaluation; excluding the two PUF evaluations, however,
the proposed scheme's remaining hash and AES operations total only
$11T_{\mathsf{hash}} + 2T_{\mathsf{aes}} = 0.107$\,ms, below LAAKA's 0.144\,ms, so
the gap is attributable entirely to hardware-rooted PUF authentication rather than
to protocol inefficiency.
Zhou et al.\ (2.608\,ms) is the heaviest, owing to the fuzzy-extractor reproduction
function ($T_{\mathsf{fe}} = 2.353$\,ms).

Combining both phases, the proposed scheme completes the full cycle in 0.605\,ms,
second only to DAuth~\cite{das2026comsnets} (0.569\,ms) among PUF-based schemes.
Both PUF-based schemes rely on four PUF evaluations
($4T_{\mathsf{puf}} = 0.480$\,ms), which dominate their total costs.
LAAKA~\cite{ali2024laaka} (0.171\,ms) achieves lower cost by forgoing PUF-based
device authentication, while Zhou et al.~\cite{zhou2024auth} (5.099\,ms) is the
heaviest, with two ECC-based fuzzy-extractor operations
($2T_{\mathsf{fe}} = 4.706$\,ms) accounting for over 92\,\% of its total cost.
The proposed scheme thus incurs only a 0.036\,ms premium over DAuth, corresponding
to four additional hash evaluations across both phases, the price paid for anonymity
and desynchronisation resilience.

\subsection{Communication Cost}

Communication cost is a core feature for analysing the performance of an
authentication scheme, based on the number of message exchanges and their sizes (in
bits).
It is assumed that identities and timestamps are 32\,bits, along with nonces, hashes,
challenges, responses, and ciphertexts as 256\,bits, ensuring a fair comparison.

\subsubsection{Enrolment Phase}

During the node enrolment phase, $D$ enrols itself with $AS$, executing three message
exchanges: (1)~$ID_D$ (32\,b) from $D$ to $AS$; (2)~$c_D$ (256\,b) and $m_D$
(256\,b) from $AS$ to $D$; (3)~$y_D$ (256\,b), $R_D$ (256\,b), and $c_{AS\text{-}D}$
(256\,b) from $D$ to $AS$.
Total enrolment communication cost: \textbf{1312\,bits}.

\subsubsection{Authentication and Key Exchange Phase}

The authentication and key exchange phase is executed using three message
communications.
Message 1 ($D \to AS$): $\mathit{PID}$ (256\,b) + $Y_{AS\text{-}D}^H$ (256\,b) +

Message 2 ($AS \to D$): $m_H$ (256\,b) + $ts_2$ (32\,b).
Message 3 ($AS \to GW$): $\mathit{PID}_{\mathrm{new}}$ (256\,b) + $ID_{AS}$ (32\,b)
+ $\mathsf{auth\_token}_D$ (256\,b).

The proposed scheme matches DAuth exactly in the enrolment phase (1312\,bits),
demonstrating that pseudonym initialisation adds no communication overhead over the
DAuth at registration.

\subsection{Storage Cost}

Storage cost captures the persistent credentials each entity must retain to run
authentication after enrolment, a decisive constraint for memory-limited IoT
endpoints.
To enable a consistent cross-scheme comparison, a single common rule is applied to
every scheme: each entity is charged with the persistent state it must keep between
sessions to authenticate again, excluding per-session ephemerals (random nonces,
timestamps, session keys) and any value regenerated on demand (PUF responses,
fuzzy-extractor secrets).
All cryptographic values---hashes, nonces, challenges, PUF responses, ciphertexts,
symmetric keys, and rotating pseudonymous identifiers---are normalised to 256\,bits,
while fixed real identities are taken as 32\,bits, in line with the
communication-cost assumptions.
The per-entity footprints obtained under these assumptions are summarised in
Table~\ref{tab:storage}.

In LAAKA~\cite{ali2024laaka}, the IoT device keeps its temporary identity $TID_d$,
the fog temporary identity $TID_f$, the masked secret $A_f$, the shared anchor $B_k$,
and its own authentication anchor $A_d = h(ID_d \| r_2)$, which it must retain to
recompute the authentication tag on every round (1280\,bits).
The fog server (FS) stores $TID_d$, $TID_f$, $A_d$, and $B_k$ (1024\,bits), while the
registration authority (RA), as the root of trust, retains $TID_d$, $TID_f$, $A_d$,
$A_f$, $B_k$, and the long-term master key $K$ (1536\,bits); it never stores the real
identity $ID_d$, as the device registers by sending only $A_d$.
In Zhou et al.~\cite{zhou2024auth}, the gateway acts as the combined authenticator
and router, maintaining per device the secret key, the identity, the dynamic
identity, the sensor identity, the real sensor identity (used to refresh the sensor
pseudonym), and a PUF challenge--response pair (1344\,bits); the user device holds its
identity, a masked credential, a helper string, the dynamic identity, and the sensor
identity (1056\,bits), while the sensor node stores only its own identifier
(256\,bits).
In DAuth~\cite{das2026comsnets}, the PUF-based device avoids long-term key storage yet
still persists its unique secret $y_D$, the challenge $c_D$, and the current session
nonce $m_{\mathrm{curr}}$ (768\,bits); the PUF response $R_D$ is regenerated on demand
from $c_D$, but $y_D$---the secret enrolled with the accumulator---is stored.
The AS retains $T_{\mathsf{Acc}}$, $ID_D$, $\Phi_{AS\text{-}D}$, $c_{AS\text{-}D}$, and
$m_{\mathrm{curr}}$ (1056\,bits), and the gateway keeps the session key
$K_{GW\text{-}D}$ and $ID_D$ (288\,bits).

The proposed scheme inherits the same device footprint as DAuth (768\,bits), because
the rotating pseudonym $\mathit{PID} = H(ID_D \| m_{\mathrm{curr}})$ is derived on the
fly rather than stored.
To support single-loss desynchronisation recovery, the AS additionally retains the
previous nonce $m_{\mathrm{old}}$ and the dual pseudonym pair
$(\mathit{PID}_{\mathrm{curr}}, \mathit{PID}_{\mathrm{old}})$ on top of the DAuth
state, raising its footprint to 1824\,bits.
Because the real identity is never exposed on the open channel, the gateway
substitutes $\mathit{PID}_{\mathrm{curr}}$ for $ID_D$ and stores it alongside
$K_{GW\text{-}D}$ (512\,bits).
As Table~\ref{tab:storage} shows, the proposed scheme and DAuth attain the lowest
device-side storage (768\,bits), roughly 40\,\% below LAAKA (1280\,bits) and 27\,\%
below Zhou et al.\ (1056\,bits), making them well-suited for memory-constrained
endpoints.
The modest additional state introduced for anonymity and desynchronisation recovery
is confined to the AS, a gateway-selected node with ample resources, and therefore
imposes no burden on the constrained device.

\subsection{Desynchronisation Recovery}
\label{sec:desync_perf}

Desynchronisation affects protocols that rotate session state after each successful
authentication: it arises when the packet delivering the updated state to one party
is lost, leaving the sender on the new state while the receiver retains the old one.
The next authentication attempt then fails from this mismatch, and the cost of
recovery depends on the fallback mechanism the scheme provides.

In DAuth~\cite{das2026comsnets}, desynchronisation is triggered when the key-delivery
packet $(m_H, ts_2)$ sent to the device at the end of the Key Exchange Phase is lost.
The AS has already advanced its nonce to $m_{\mathrm{new}}$ while the device retains
$m_{\mathrm{curr}}$.
As DAuth stores only a single nonce state, the device's next attempt uses the stale
$m_{\mathrm{curr}}$ and produces a pseudonym the server cannot recognise, so the
request is rejected and recovery demands a complete re-enrolment, re-executing the
PUF challenge--response exchange and reconstructing the hash-accumulator credential
before authentication can resume.
The proposed scheme shares the same protocol structure and is exposed to the
identical trigger, but its AS retains a dual state,
$(m_{\mathrm{curr}}, \mathit{PID}_{\mathrm{curr}})$ and
$(m_{\mathrm{old}}, \mathit{PID}_{\mathrm{old}})$.
When the device re-authenticates with the stale pseudonym
$\mathit{PID}_{\mathrm{old}}$, the server recognises it via the dual-state lookup and
completes a fresh authentication and key exchange without re-enrolment.

Both LAAKA~\cite{ali2024laaka} and Zhou et al.~\cite{zhou2024auth} also rotate their
anonymous identifiers, but each retains the previous identifier rather than a single
drifting secret.
LAAKA derives $TID_{d,\mathrm{new}}$ from a fresh random each session while the fog
server keeps the prior $TID_d$, and Zhou et al.\ have the gateway \emph{add} the new
$DID_{i,\mathrm{new}}$ to the user record while \emph{retaining} the previous $DID_i$.
In both schemes a device that misses the final message is therefore still recognised
on its prior identifier and re-synchronises through an ordinary authentication round,
without re-registration.
As neither holds a single rotating secret that can drift irreconcilably between the
parties, a like-for-like desynchronisation-recovery scenario does not apply to either,
and both are excluded.
The evaluation therefore contrasts DAuth and the proposed scheme only, which share
the same trigger and threat model but differ in their recovery mechanism.

Figure~\ref{fig:desync_bar} reports the per-device cost of a recovery round, taken
immediately after a desynchronisation event, over a 100-node RPL network (20 devices,
5 seeds).
In DAuth, recovery requires a failed authentication, a full re-enrolment, and a fresh
authentication and key exchange (134.8\,mJ, 2.18\,s).
The proposed dual-state lookup instead recovers with a single re-authentication and
key exchange (87.2\,mJ, 1.41\,s), cutting per-device recovery energy and CPU time by
\textbf{35.3\,\%} over DAuth.

\section{Hardware-Based Performance Analysis}
\label{sec:hw}

Hardware-based evaluation validates the feasibility of the proposed scheme by
measuring its performance on physical devices under real operating conditions.

All four schemes (the proposed scheme, DAuth~\cite{das2026comsnets},
LAAKA~\cite{ali2024laaka}, and Zhou et al.~\cite{zhou2024auth}) are implemented on a
\textbf{Raspberry Pi~4B} platform, equipped with a quad-core 64-bit ARM Cortex-A72
processor operating at 1.5\,GHz, using the MIRACL Core cryptographic library for all
hash, AES, and elliptic-curve operations.
Identical hardware is used across all four schemes to ensure a fair comparison of
energy consumption and CPU time.
The implementation considers two Raspberry Pi~4B platforms as IoT nodes: one
performing the role of the newly joined device, and the other functioning as the
authentication server.
A Windows laptop is leveraged as the gateway.
Each of the three entities maintains direct TCP communication with the others.
Energy consumption is estimated using the active power profile of the Raspberry
Pi~4B (3.8\,W) multiplied by the measured wall-clock time per authentication round.
The physical testbed is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:hw_setup}.

\begin{figure}[!ht]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth,height=0.52\textwidth,keepaspectratio]{HW_setup_signals}
  \caption{Hardware testbed: two Raspberry Pi~4B boards (left: authentication
           server; right: device) connected to a Windows laptop (gateway).}
  \label{fig:hw_setup}
\end{figure}

As illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:hw_comparison}(a), the proposed scheme
(\textbf{0.0617\,J} per round) and DAuth~\cite{das2026comsnets}
(\textbf{0.0611\,J}) achieve virtually identical energy consumption, differing by
less than \textbf{1\,\%}.
This confirms that the additional SHA-256 evaluations for pseudonym rotation and the
dual-state nonce lookup are sub-millisecond relative to the network round-trip time
and introduce no measurable overhead over the non-anonymous baseline.
Both outperform LAAKA~\cite{ali2024laaka} (0.0959\,J) by approximately
\textbf{36\,\%} and Zhou et al.~\cite{zhou2024auth} (0.1074\,J) by approximately
\textbf{43\,\%} in energy consumption.
The higher cost of Zhou et al.\ arises from a nested gateway-to-sensor-node
sub-exchange embedded within the authentication round that extends the effective
device-side round-trip, whereas LAAKA's two consecutive round-trips carry only
lightweight hash-based payloads and therefore complete faster in absolute terms.

The CPU time results in Figure~\ref{fig:hw_comparison}(b) follow the same ordering
with identical percentage margins, since energy is directly proportional to
wall-clock time under a fixed active power draw: the proposed scheme (0.0162\,s) and
DAuth (0.0161\,s) perform equivalently, both remaining below LAAKA (0.0253\,s) and
Zhou et al.\ (0.0282\,s) by approximately 36\,\% and 43\,\%, respectively.
The ordering of schemes is consistent between the COOJA simulation and the hardware
evaluation, confirming that the proposed scheme's advantage reflects protocol design
rather than platform-specific effects.
The hardware evaluation thus validates that provable anonymity is achieved at no
measurable cost penalty over the non-anonymous baseline on real IoT devices.
